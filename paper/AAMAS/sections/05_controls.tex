\section{Two things the cooperation is not}
\label{sec:controls}

Section~\ref{sec:riskgrid} leaves two cheap explanations standing. The models may be
repeating a solution the prompt handed them, since a dilemma stated with a target, a group
size and a round count also states its own equal split; or they may not understand the game
well enough for the risk to mean anything. We test both on the same panel, at
$p\in\{\PanelNohintRisks\}$, and Table~\ref{tab:controls} reports them side by side.

\subsection{Deleting the focal point}
\label{sec:nohint}

The prompt states the equal split twice: once as a gloss on the target (``an average of
$\PanelFairShare$ per player per round'') and once as a worked example on the payoff line
(``contributing $\PanelFairShare$ every round leaves you $\PanelFairTotal$ at the end'').
We delete both spans and change nothing else, which leaves the target, the group size and
the round count in place, so the split remains derivable by one division. Each model plays
$\PanelNohintGames$ games with the spans deleted against the $\PanelNohintGames$ baseline
games at the same risk levels.

Contribution never falls. \PanelNohintUpWordCap{} models rise significantly
(\PanelNohintUpModels{}) and \PanelNohintFlatWord{} do not move at all
(\PanelNohintFlatModels{}); \PanelNohintDownWord{} contributes less. The two largest
moves are the ones that matter, because both go the wrong way for the
instruction-following account. Qwen goes from $\PanelNohintBaseQwen$
units to $\PanelNohintAfterQwen$ ($P~\PanelNohintPQwen$) and its target rate from
$\PanelNohintReachBaseQwen\%$ to $\PanelNohintReachAfterQwen\%$, yet its final cash falls
from $\PanelNohintPayBaseQwen$ to $\PanelNohintPayAfterQwen$: it reaches the goal by
spending most of its endowment on it. Grok goes from
$\PanelNohintBaseGrok$ to $\PanelNohintAfterGrok$ of $\PanelEndowment$
($P~\PanelNohintPGrok$), the share of its seats contributing the maximum in every round
rising from $\PanelNohintBaseExactFourGrok\%$ to $\PanelNohintExactFourGrok\%$, and its
cash falls to $\PanelNohintPayAfterGrok$. Removing the sentence that names a moderate
contribution does not remove the cooperation; it removes the ceiling on it.

The models that do not move show where the split actually comes from.
Flash-Lite plays exactly $\PanelFairShare$ in every round of
$\PanelNohintExactTwoFlash\%$ of its seats without the gloss, against
$\PanelNohintBaseExactTwoFlash\%$ with it, and Luna moves by
$\PanelNohintDeltaLuna$ units ($P=\PanelNohintPLuna$). They reconstruct the split rather
than reciting it, which they can: $\PanelTarget$ over six players over ten rounds is one
division. The equal split is therefore a focal point of the \emph{game}, not a phrase in
the prompt, and deleting the phrase leaves the behaviour it was supposed to explain intact
while making two models worse off.

\subsection{Asking the agent what it knows}
\label{sec:evprobe}

Between rounds we put questions to the same agent that is playing, in a separate call whose
answer never enters the game history, so a probe cannot steer the play it measures. Each
game carries $\PanelProbePerGame$ questions at rounds $\PanelProbeRounds$, giving
$\PanelProbeN$ in total: $\PanelProbeRulesN$ on the mechanics --- the endowment, the target,
the legal contributions, the round count, the stated risk, the payoff after a disaster ---
and the rest on value. The value question we rely on names both strategies in words and
asks which pays more on average: contributing $\PanelFairShare$ every round and finishing
with a certain $\PanelFairTotal$, or contributing nothing and keeping $\PanelEndowment$
unless the disaster strikes.

Comprehension is not the obstacle. The panel answers all $\PanelProbeRulesN$ rule
questions correctly, $\PanelProbeRulesAcc\%$, with no model below it. On the value
comparison the panel scores $\PanelProbeCompareAcc\%$ overall and
$\PanelProbeCompareLowAcc\%$ at $p=\PanelProbeLowRisk$, where the right answer is that
contributing nothing pays more --- $\PanelProbeLowOptCash$ against $\PanelFairTotal$. The
aggregate hides a distinction the same data settles. \PanelProbeConstantModels{} return the
same answer at every risk level (Qwen gives one value in
$\PanelProbeModalShareQwen\%$ of its answers), so their score at
$p=\PanelProbeLowRisk$ is a coincidence and not a computation. The other
\PanelProbeCompareLowPerfectWord{} models change their
answer with $p$ and are right each time: $\PanelProbeCompareHaiku\%$ for
Haiku across all three levels, $\PanelProbeCompareFlash\%$ for
Flash-Lite and $\PanelProbeCompareLuna\%$ for Luna.

Those three then play against their own answer. In the very games in which they state that
contributing nothing pays more, they contribute $\PanelProbeLowContribHaiku$,
$\PanelProbeLowContribFlash$ and $\PanelProbeLowContribLuna$ units respectively, which is
the equal split to within a rounding error in all three cases. The comparison is
within-agent and within-game rather than across conditions, so no difference in prompt,
sampling or model version can absorb it. Whatever produces the contribution, it is not
reached by evaluating the two strategies the agent has just evaluated correctly.
