\section{Watching the pool, not the players}
\label{sec:mixed}

Every table so far has held one model in all six seats, or one model against five fixed
scripts. Both leave the same question open, and it is the question that matters for a
system of several agents. An agent that always pays its fair share cannot be told apart
from an agent that watches its partners closely and keeps finding no reason to change.
To separate those two, the partners have to be worth watching. So we seat two different
models at the same table.

\paragraph{Design.}
All \EthreebPairs{} pairs of the \EthreebModels{} models play together. Each pair plays at
\EthreebMixtures{} mixtures, from one seat of the first model up to five, at
\EthreebRisks{} catastrophe probabilities, with \EthreebReps{} repetitions of each cell.
The design balances itself: in a round robin over all pairs, every model sits in the same
number of games and fills the same number of seats as every other, so no comparison here
rests on one model having been measured more often. Positions rotate as well, so a model's
identity is never tied to where it sits.

\paragraph{The response to partners, and the part of it that is not real.}
For each seat we ask a simple question. When the other five seats contributed more in the
previous round, does this seat contribute more in the next one? We use the previous round
because all six decide at the same time, so a comparison inside one round would measure
resemblance rather than response. We compare within a game and within a round, which
removes the catastrophe probability, the pair, and the mixture in one step, since all
three are fixed for the whole game. Standard errors treat one game as one unit, because
six seats across ten rounds of the same game share a history, a pool and a lottery draw.

Read raw, three models give less when their partners gave more, which reads as free
riding. For two of them it is an artifact of the threshold. The pool before a round is
the pool before the previous one, plus what the seat itself gave, plus five times the
partners' mean. The partners' mean is therefore part of the pool, and an agent steering
only toward the threshold will give less after its partners give more, with no interest
in the partners at all. Holding the pool fixed asks whether it still matters who filled
it. Table~\ref{tab:mixed-recip} reports both, and the gap between the two columns is the
size of the threshold channel.

Net of the pool, \ErecipNBlind{} of the \EthreebModels{} models answer their partners
with nothing a permutation test can separate from zero, or with less than a quarter of a
contribution step, which on a choice set of 0, 2 and 4 is not a faint response but no
response. The remaining \ErecipNReal{} is \ErecipRealModels, and it runs the wrong way:
it gives less to partners who give more. The others are watching a running total rather
than the agents producing it, and the one that watches the agents exploits them.

\paragraph{Company changes one model, and only in the aggregate.}
The same data answers a second and coarser question. Does a model contribute differently
when more of its own kind fill the table? Here one model does separate itself.
Averaged over a whole game, \EcompTopModel{} rises by \EcompTopSpan{} units as its own
kind fills more of the table, about \EcompRatio{} times the largest movement any other
model shows, and the other four stay flat. The per-mixture means are in the supplementary
material.

The two questions come apart, and that is the useful part. The model with the largest
effect of company has no response to its partners inside a game, and the models with a
detectable within-game response barely move with company at all. A study that measured
only one of the two would have named a different model as the social one, and would have
been wrong either way. Company shifts an average; it does not mean anyone is watching.
