\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Collective-risk dilemmas.}
The game we use was introduced to study why groups fail to avert a shared
catastrophe~\cite{milinski2008collective}: \PanelNplayersWord{} players contribute from private endowments
towards a threshold, and missing it triggers a loss with a probability the experimenter
sets. Human groups reach the target reliably only when the probability is high, and
inequality between players and the ability to communicate both shift the
outcome~\cite{tavoni2011inequality}. Evolutionary treatments explain the pattern by the
same threshold structure we exploit here: risk of collective loss turns a defection
equilibrium into a coordination problem with multiple equilibria, and which one a
population reaches depends on the risk~\cite{santos2011risk}. That literature establishes
the game as a place where behaviour \emph{should} move with $p$, which is what makes a
flat response diagnostic rather than merely disappointing. Our contribution is not a new
account of human play but a measurement instrument applied to artificial players.

\paragraph{Social dilemmas in multiagent systems.}
Sequential social dilemmas gave multiagent reinforcement learning a setting in which
cooperation is a policy property rather than a matrix
entry~\cite{leibo2017multi,perolat2017multi}, and subsequent work showed that changing the
learner's objective --- inequity aversion, for instance --- changes which equilibrium a
population settles into~\cite{hughes2018inequity}. The programme of cooperative AI states
the design question directly: build agents that can cooperate with agents unlike
themselves~\cite{dafoe2020open}. Language-model agents enter that programme with a
property no learned policy has, in that their behaviour is fixed at deployment rather than
shaped by the population they meet, and the present paper measures what that fixed
behaviour is.

\paragraph{Language-model agents in games.}
Language models have been placed in canonical games and read as economic
subjects~\cite{horton2023large}, played against each other and against scripted strategies
in two-player repeated games~\cite{akata2023playing,fontana2024nicer}, and embedded in
societies that manage a shared resource~\cite{piatti2024cooperate} or an open-ended social
world~\cite{park2023generative}. Two design choices recur. Beyond two players the seats
usually hold copies of one model, which leaves the agent's response to a \emph{different}
policy unmeasured; and the reported quantity is usually an outcome, such as whether the
resource survived, rather than a distance from a computed optimum. We keep the game and
change both choices: opponents are fixed policies chosen so that the best response can be
enumerated, and every result is reported against that best response.

\paragraph{Overlap with concurrent work.}
A concurrent manuscript uses the same game and an overlapping panel to ask whether
language models reproduce the risk sensitivity of human subjects, and reports the
single-model baseline that Section~\ref{sec:riskgrid} re-runs and extends~\cite{anon-if}.
The questions differ: that work asks whether a model is risk-sensitive, and treats the
answer as a statement about machine behaviour; the present paper asks what a model does
when the other agents are not copies of itself, and treats the answer as a statement about
deploying agent populations. The results in Sections~\ref{sec:controls}
and~\ref{sec:bestresponse}, which carry this paper, have no counterpart there.
