\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A language-model agent that manages a budget, a shared queue or a resource allocation is a
player in a social dilemma whether or not its designer framed it as one. Its choices carry
a cost to itself and a benefit to a group, and the group may fail. What a designer needs to
know before deploying such an agent is narrow and practical: does it defend the resources
it was given, and does what it does depend on what the other agents do? An agent that
contributes whatever the situation is not a cooperator but a liability, since a single
opportunistic counterpart converts its generosity into a transfer.

Answering either question is exactly what the standard experimental design cannot do.
Studies of language-model agents in social dilemmas commonly seat copies of a single
model at every position~\cite{piatti2024cooperate,park2023generative}, and where the design
does vary the opponent it is usually a two-player repeated game~\cite{akata2023playing,%
fontana2024nicer}. Self-play with more than two seats produces a transcript that a model
reasoning about its opponents and a model executing a fixed contribution schedule generate
alike, so the observation ``the agents cooperated'' is compatible with both, and only one
of them survives contact with an opponent who defects. The prompt compounds the problem: a
dilemma stated with a group target and a round count states its own equal split, and an
agent that repeats that split scores as a cooperator on every aggregate measure.

We break the symmetry of self-play in a collective-risk social
dilemma~\cite{milinski2008collective}, where \PanelNplayersWord{} players contribute from private
endowments towards a group target and a catastrophe lottery fires with probability $p$ if
they miss it. The game suits the question because $p$ is a dial the experimenter owns. It moves the
optimum from ``contribute nothing'' to ``contribute exactly your share'' at a threshold we
derive in closed form, so an agent that is choosing and an agent that is repeating are
told apart by whether anything changes when the optimum does. We run $\PanelNgamesPanel$ panel/control games on \PanelNmodelsWord{} production models, one per provider
and all at the cheap tier of their family. Three controls follow, and each removes one explanation
for the cooperation the models display; we then add a mixed-population test of whether agents
condition on who contributes.

The first control varies the risk itself over an \PanelNrisksWord{}-point grid whose endpoint,
$p=0$, removes the catastrophe altogether and thereby makes contributing strictly
dominated: no belief and no attitude to risk can justify a single unit. The second deletes
the two spans of the prompt that state the equal split, which tests whether the models are
solving the dilemma or reciting the instructions. The third seats one model against $\EthreeaxSeatsScripted$
opponents whose policies we fix in advance, which makes the best response an arithmetic
fact rather than an estimate. Alongside all three we ask the playing agent, in the middle
of its own game, what the rules are and which of two named strategies pays more, so that a
shortfall in play can be attributed to knowledge or denied it. Finally, E6 repeats the two
diagnostic endpoint cells under two prompt paraphrases and temperature-zero decoding.

Our contributions are the following.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A risk-response measurement with a control that needs no assumptions
  (\S\ref{sec:riskgrid}).} We characterise the equilibria of the game as a function of $p$
  and show that welfare-optimal and equilibrium play switch at the same threshold,
  $p^{*}=\PanelPstar$. Against that benchmark \PanelNFlatWord{} of \PanelNmodelsWord{} models
  contribute a constant across all $\PanelNrisks$ risk levels, the fourth changes only at
  $p=0$, and $\PanelZeroSeatsPaying$ of $\PanelZeroSeats$ seats pay at $p=0$, where paying
  is strictly dominated. The panel forfeits $\PanelForfeitPanelPct\%$ of attainable payoff
  (Figure~\ref{fig:grid}).

\item \textbf{Two controls that rule out the cheap explanations (\S\ref{sec:controls}).}
  Deleting the prompt's equal-split gloss never lowers contribution and raises it to the
  endowment cap in two models, so the behaviour is not compliance with a supplied focal
  point. The agents answer $\PanelProbeRulesN$ in-situ questions about the rules at
  $\PanelProbeRulesAcc\%$ and, at $p=\PanelProbeLowRisk$, identify contributing nothing as
  the higher-paying strategy at $\PanelProbeCompareLowAcc\%$. They then contribute
  $\PanelProbeLowContrib$ units in the same games. Knowing the answer and acting on it come
  apart.

\item \textbf{A best-response profile against scripted opponents
  (\S\ref{sec:bestresponse}).} With $\EthreeaxSeatsScripted$ scripted seats the best response is computable
  exactly given the experimenter's policy specification. No model best-responds in the
  $\EthreeaDominatedGames$ information-set-safe games in which contributing is strictly
  dominated; across all $\EthreeaNgames$ games, $\EthreeaOptimalGames$ are exactly optimal
  under the risk-neutral benchmark.

\item \textbf{Opponent conditioning in mixed populations (\S\ref{sec:mixed}).}
  After controlling for the running pool and the seat's previous action, four models show
  no behaviorally meaningful partner response; the remaining response is negative.
\end{itemize}

Read together, the diagnostic layers say that the cooperation these agents display is mostly
a schedule rather than a choice, although E6 leaves a model-specific risk response intact.
A schedule makes them safe
collaborators and unsafe stakeholders, and that distinction is one a deployment can design
around only once it is measured.
