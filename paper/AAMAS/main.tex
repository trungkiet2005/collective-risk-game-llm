\title{When Low-Cost Language Models Cooperate by Default: Incentives, Partners, and Selection in a Collective-Risk Dilemma}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affiliation{\institution{Anonymous Institution}\country{}}

%% Keep in sync, word for word, with OPENREVIEW_ABSTRACT.txt. No result numbers in the abstract.
\begin{abstract}
Language-model agents increasingly act in shared environments where one local choice can expose a whole group to collective loss. We study whether cooperation follows the stakes, the partners, or an unspoken default. In a collective-risk social dilemma, agents contribute to a shared threshold over repeated rounds; a catastrophe destroys savings when the threshold is missed. We evaluate five low-cost production models through self-play, prompt controls, value questions, scripted partners, mixed tables, and payoff-based selection.

The main result is a dissociation between cooperative-looking play and incentive response. Four models contribute near the equal share at every positive risk level, and most models also pay at zero risk, where contribution cannot help. Removing normative wording leaves this default largely intact, whereas stating that only the agent's own cash matters reduces payment at zero risk for most models. Several models answer risk comparisons correctly in separate questions but do not change their actions. Against fixed partners, no model consistently follows the exact best response, and some continue paying after the outcome is settled. In mixed tables, one late under-contributor can break an otherwise successful group, while payoff selection among tablemates rewards the smallest payer and harms group success. At moderate and high risk, selection across independent groups instead favours an exact fair-share policy.

These findings show why self-play success is not enough to evaluate multiagent systems. Evaluations should state the agent's objective, compare behaviour with a computed optimum, test situations where actions are dominated, and measure performance in mixed populations.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{LLM agents, collective-risk social dilemma, cooperation, best response, empirical game-theoretic analysis, multiagent evaluation}

Language-model agents are starting to act for people in shared settings: they spend budgets, share compute and bargain over common resources. Each such agent is a player in a social dilemma, because what it pays helps the group and costs its owner. Before deploying one, a designer needs to know two things. Does the agent's cooperation depend on what is at stake? And does it depend on what the other agents do?

Self-play, a common test, cannot answer either question. When every seat holds a copy of the same model, an agent that reasons about the stakes and an agent that repeats a fixed rule can leave the same record of play. A success rate also hides how far the agents were from the best they could have done: a group can reach its goal and still waste most of its money. And a prompt that gives a group target and a number of rounds already suggests an equal split, so an agent that simply repeats that split looks cooperative on every summary measure.

We study both questions in a classic game from research on climate cooperation, the collective-risk social dilemma~\cite{milinski2008collective}. Players pay into a shared pool over several rounds, and if the pool misses a target, a catastrophe destroys everyone's savings with probability $p$. The best outcome for the group has a simple shape. For risk-neutral players, it is to pay nothing while the risk is below one half, and above it for everyone to pay the \emph{fair share}, the target split equally. When there is no risk at all, paying only lowers a player's own money, so no attitude to risk can explain it.

We call play a \emph{default} when it does not change with what is at stake. The game offers two sharp tests of a default, because in both situations paying cannot help: paying when there is no risk, and paying after the outcome can no longer change. We test five low-cost production models, one per provider: Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna, Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20. We use four designs. Self-play covers the whole range of risk. Prompt controls ask the agent which choice pays more, remove normative words, or state the agent's goal. One model plays beside scripted partners, where the best response can be computed exactly. And two different models share a table, which lets us ask which agent would win if agents were selected by payoff~\cite{wellman2006methods,tuyls2018generalised}. We report four findings (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Risk does not change the default} (Section~\ref{sec:risk}). Most models pay about the fair share at every positive risk level, and most also pay when there is no risk. Removing normative words changes neither result. Stating that only the agent's own cash matters cuts most zero-risk payment, but only GPT-5.6 Luna then moves from paying little at low risk to the fair share at high risk.
\item \textbf{Answers track the risk; play does not} (Section~\ref{sec:knowing}). Asked in separate calls, several models rank the two choices correctly and change that ranking with the risk, but they keep paying the same amount. Beside scripted partners who make every payment a loss, no model keeps all its money, and two models keep paying after the outcome is settled.
\item \textbf{An exact fair share has no slack} (Section~\ref{sec:groups}). One partner that stops paying in the last round often breaks a table of exact fair-share players, while a table that overpays absorbs it. Most agents pay less when generous partners fill most of the table.
\item \textbf{The scoring rule decides who is selected} (Section~\ref{sec:selection}). Tablemates share the pool and the lottery, so scoring agents against their tablemates favours the smallest payer and lowers group success, while scoring them across separate groups favours the exact fair-share player at moderate and high risk.
\end{itemize}

For the models we test, payment is a default rather than a response to the stakes. A default can work well beside partners like itself, and how a system states its agents' goals and selects its agents can make its failures better or worse.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Collective risk and selection.}
The collective-risk dilemma was built to study why groups fail to avoid a shared disaster~\cite{milinski2008collective}. Human groups reach the target mostly when the risk is high~\cite{milinski2008collective}, and inequality and communication change the result~\cite{tavoni2011inequality}. Games with a threshold have many equilibria~\cite{palfrey1984participation}, human contributions decline over the rounds of repeated public goods games~\cite{andreoni1988free}, and the risk of collective failure can turn a defection trap into a coordination problem~\cite{santos2011risk}. Theory also shows that selection on payoff relative to a small group favours whoever does better than its neighbours~\cite{schaffer1988evolutionarily}, while selection between groups can favour cooperation~\cite{traulsen2006evolution}. This literature tells us that behaviour \emph{should} move with the risk, so a flat response is informative, and we reuse its selection theory to read our mixed tables.

\paragraph{Language models in games.}
Language models have been studied as economic subjects~\cite{horton2023large,chen2023emergence,mei2024turing}, in repeated two-player games~\cite{akata2025playing,fontana2024nicer}, and in multi-player game benchmarks, where contributions to a public good can stay the same even when it returns nothing~\cite{huang2025competing}. Models tend to be more cooperative than people~\cite{mei2024turing}, an even split can reflect a focal point rather than fairness~\cite{dodivers2025uncovering}, and models can hold a correct belief or plan and act against it~\cite{fan2024can,schmied2026llms}. In social dilemmas, societies of agents over-use a common resource~\cite{piatti2024cooperate}, reasoning models free-ride in public goods games~\cite{guzmanpiedrahita2025corrupted}, and agents stand in for people in a collective-risk game~\cite{slumbers2025using}. A study of 35 open models includes a collective-risk game at one risk level and finds play far from the group optimum~\cite{kumar2026cooperative}. Willis et al.\ study a binary collective-risk game with strategies written by three models and find that payoff-biased imitation drives larger groups to selfish, low-welfare outcomes~\cite{willis2026evaluating}. In another study, prompts that evolve under individual selection collapse into defection while group selection keeps them prosocial~\cite{celiktemel2026group}. We differ in three ways. We vary the risk from zero to certainty and compare turn-by-turn play with a computed optimum, adding two tests where paying cannot help. We compute the exact best response to fixed partners. And we select among deployed models from their real mixed tables, showing that the switch from tablemate to across-table scoring happens at seven or eight agents.

\paragraph{Multiagent evaluation.}
Multiagent learning and cooperative AI ask for agents that work with partners unlike themselves~\cite{leibo2017multi,hughes2018inequity,dafoe2020open,stone2010ad,leibo2021scalable}. Empirical game-theoretic analysis treats trained agents as strategies of a meta-game~\cite{wellman2006methods,tuyls2018generalised}, and $\alpha$-Rank ranks them by evolutionary dynamics~\cite{omidshafiei2019alpha}. Tournaments that mix language models with classic strategies report model-specific strategic fingerprints in the prisoner's dilemma~\cite{payne2025strategic}, cooperation evolves differently across base models in a donor game~\cite{vallinder2025cultural}, and cooperation sustained by repetition weakens when co-players differ~\cite{tewolde2026coopeval}. In theory, adaptive players in a collective-risk game compensate for the effort of artificial agents~\cite{terrucha2024art}. We add a many-player dilemma in which the best response to fixed partners can be computed exactly, and measure this compensation with real models.

\section{Game-theoretic benchmark and evaluation design}
\label{sec:game}

\subsection{Rules}
Six players each start with $e=40$ units, and the game lasts $R=10$ rounds. In each round every player privately chooses a contribution $a_i^t\in\{0,2,4\}$ to a shared \emph{pool}, and after each round all players see what every seat has paid. Let $x_i=\sum_t a_i^t$ be a player's total and $X=\sum_i x_i$ the pool. The target is $T=120$. A player keeps what it did not pay, but if the pool misses the target, a catastrophe destroys all savings with probability $p$ (Table~\ref{tab:terms} lists the terms we use):
\begin{equation}
u_i \;=\;
\begin{cases}
e-x_i & \text{if } X\ge T,\\
0 \text{ with probability } p, \text{ else } e-x_i & \text{if } X<T.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:payoff}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:eq}
Consider the game in which each player picks a total $x_i\in\{0,2,\dots,40\}$, and let $p<1$. A profile is a Nash equilibrium if and only if $x=0$, or $X=T$ and $x_i\le pe$ for every $i$. A target-reaching equilibrium exists if and only if $p\ge\popt=T/(6e)=\tfrac12$. The welfare-optimal outcome is $x=0$ for $p<\popt$ and $X=T$ for $p>\popt$, and both are optimal at $p=\popt$, so the welfare-optimal payoff per player is $\mathrm{opt}(p)=\max\{(1-p)e,\;e-T/6\}$; this welfare statement also holds at $p=1$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} If $X<T$, any player with $x_i>0$ gains by paying nothing, so the only equilibrium that misses the target is $x=0$. If $X>T$, then $X\ge T+2$ because totals are even, so a player can pay 2 less and still reach the target. If $X=T$, the best deviation is to pay nothing, worth $(1-p)e$ against $e-x_i$, which gives $x_i\le pe$; summing over players gives $p\ge T/(6e)$, and conversely $x_i=20$ for every $i$ is an equilibrium when $p\ge\tfrac12$. Welfare is $6e-X$ when the target is met and $(1-p)(6e-X)$ otherwise, so it peaks at $X=T$ or $X=0$. The ten-round game, in which strategies can react to the history, has the same totals in pure-strategy Nash equilibrium (full proof in the supplementary material); we claim nothing about subgame perfection, and $\mathrm{opt}(p)$ is a welfare bound that does not depend on the equilibrium concept.\hfill$\square$

\paragraph{What we compare play with.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:eq} gives the \emph{optimum} for risk-neutral players: keep everything below the pivot and pay the fair share above it. The optimum is a benchmark, not a prediction, since paying nothing is an equilibrium at every risk level. Paying is dominated in two situations: at $p=0$, where both cases of \eqref{eq:payoff} give the same payoff, and once the outcome is settled. Neither test depends on the agent's attitude to risk or on its beliefs about the others. Payment that continues in these two situations is therefore the sharpest evidence of a default.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:table}
For two seats $i$ and $j$ in the same game, the difference in their expected payoffs over the lottery is $c\,(x_j-x_i)$, with $c=1$ if the target was reached and $c=1-p$ otherwise.
\end{proposition}

\noindent Both seats face the same pool and the same lottery, so only their own payments differ. Hence, for $p<1$, whoever pays less at a table earns more, whatever the group achieves.

\paragraph{Prompts.}
In each round a seat receives one prompt with the rules, the full history of every seat's payments, and a request for one contribution; the supplementary material gives every prompt word for word. The base prompt calls the pool a ``climate account'', says that the group ``must'' reach the target, calls the loss a ``disaster'' and names the game as a collective-risk social dilemma. It never states the player's goal, and it hints at the equal split twice. The prompt controls change exactly these parts. \emph{No hint} deletes the two equal-split phrases. \emph{Neutral wording} replaces the normative and naming phrases with plain ones, such as ``a shared account''. \emph{Goal stated} keeps the neutral wording and adds an opening that says the player is in a paid experiment where ``the only thing that matters to you is your own final cash payoff''. Two \emph{rewordings} change the phrasing but keep every rule and the hint.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
The running pool is not printed, so a seat that wants it must add up the history. The prompt is in English, no seat is given a persona, and the temperature is 0.7 unless stated. We read the move from the reply's final \texttt{CONTRIBUTION:} line. A reply that is cut off at the output limit of 3,000 tokens is asked again with a larger limit rather than guessed, and all but \CntParseFail{} of the \CntDecisions{} replies could be read.

\paragraph{Measures.}
We report \emph{expected} payoffs, the average a seat would earn if the catastrophe lottery were run many times on the same payments, and the share of $\mathrm{opt}(p)$ that is lost. Proposition~\ref{prop:eq}, $\mathrm{opt}(p)$ and the selection fitness below are all defined in expected payoffs, and given the payments the expected payoff removes the lottery noise without bias. Agents never see the lottery draw, and the supplementary material lists the realised draws. The unit of analysis is the game, since seats in one game share a history and a lottery. Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstraps over games, and tests are permutation tests over games. The supplementary material gives every value behind the statements below.

\section{Risk does not change the default}
\label{sec:risk}

\centering

Four of the five models pay about the fair share at every positive risk level (Figure~\ref{fig:selfplay}a). Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna and Qwen3-235B pay almost exactly the same amount below the pivot as above it (a step of at most \GridStepMaxOther{} units), while a risk-neutral player would jump from nothing to the fair share and human groups with the same rules reach the target in half of their games at $p=0.9$ and in none at $p=0.1$~\cite{milinski2008collective}. Strong risk aversion could explain a flat line above zero risk, since a catastrophe leaves nothing and a player with relative risk aversion near one already prefers a certain fair share at low risk. No attitude to risk explains paying when there is no risk at all, and neither does altruism, since that payment helps nobody. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite is the clearest case. Almost every one of its seats plays the exact split at every risk level (Table~\ref{tab:models}), so its play is optimal above the pivot only because a fixed rule happens to match the optimum there.

\paragraph{Paying when there is nothing to protect.}
Four of the five models also pay at $p=0$, about as much as at any other risk level, although paying there only lowers a player's own money (Table~\ref{tab:models}, first column). GPT-5.6 Luna is the exception. It pays nothing at $p=0$ and its usual share at every positive risk, so it tells no risk apart from some risk but does not respond to how large the risk is.

\paragraph{Three ways to lose.}
The models lose payoff in three different ways (Table~\ref{tab:models}, Figure~\ref{fig:selfplay}c). Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.6 Luna lose mostly at low risk, where they pay for a target that is not worth reaching. Grok 4.20 overpays: its groups reach the target well before the last round, and its seats keep paying into a pool that is already safe. Qwen3-235B fails at the end. It pays 2 round after round, then almost all of its seats pay nothing in the last round (Figure~\ref{fig:selfplay}b), so its groups almost never reach the target; had each seat simply repeated its previous move, every one of those groups would have succeeded. The skip follows the clock, not the pool, much as human contributions decline at the end of repeated public goods games~\cite{andreoni1988free}: across all games with the base prompt, Qwen3-235B skips the last payment as often when its payment is needed as when the others' fair shares already suffice (\StratQwenZeroPivotal\% of seats in both cases), and it almost never stops in an earlier round once the target is met. GPT-5.6 Luna fails in the opposite way: its last move depends on whether its payment is needed. It skips in \StratLunaZeroPivotal\% of cases when the target needs its payment, but in \StratLunaZeroSlack\% when the others' fair shares would suffice. When six copies of this rule each count on another seat to pay, tables that arrive ahead of the fair pace of 12 per round often miss the target, while tables exactly on pace reach it. Claude Haiku 4.5 shows that the pool can be read from the same prompt, since its seats stop paying in the last round mostly when the target has already been reached. Success rates hide all of this. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Grok 4.20 both reach the target in every game, yet Grok 4.20 keeps far less of its money. For $0.5<p<1$, Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20 groups earn less in most games than in the worst equilibrium of Proposition~\ref{prop:eq}, in which nobody pays.

\paragraph{The default survives rewording, but its failures do not.}
Deleting the two equal-split phrases lowers no model's payment (Table~\ref{tab:robust}), so the hint set a ceiling and guided precision but was not the source of the cooperation. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite still plays the exact split, which it can rebuild from the target, while GPT-5.6 Luna keeps its average payment but plays the exact split less often. Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20 pay more without the hint; Qwen3-235B now reaches the target, but it earns less because it overpays. The two rewordings and a temperature of zero leave the payments of the four models other than Grok 4.20 almost unchanged. The failures are less stable than the averages. Under the second rewording Grok 4.20 pays about the fair share, and how often Qwen3-235B drops out in the last round swings widely from one prompt to another. Grok 4.20's small rise with risk on the grid mostly disappears in the rerun, so we do not read it as a response to risk.

\paragraph{Stating the goal, not removing the norms, cuts payment at zero risk.}
The default does not come from the normative words. With neutral wording, most models still pay at $p=0$, and at low and high risk four models pay on average what they paid before, although GPT-5.6 Luna plays the exact split less often, while Grok 4.20 pays even more (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). Adding the stated goal is what changes play at zero risk: Claude Haiku 4.5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite then pay almost nothing there, and Qwen3-235B pays far less than before, while Grok 4.20 barely changes. The goal still does not make most models weigh the risk. Claude Haiku 4.5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite pay the fair share already at low risk, so they tell no risk apart from some risk but do not grade it, a pattern that strong risk aversion could also produce. Only GPT-5.6 Luna moves from paying little at low risk to paying the fair share at high risk. Qwen3-235B pays somewhat more at high risk than at low risk, but less than before at every risk level, and never reaches the target; Grok 4.20 keeps paying far more than needed. Payment at zero risk therefore depends on leaving the goal unstated for most models, while the flat response above zero risk does not. \section{Answers track the risk; play does not}
\label{sec:knowing}

Perhaps the agents simply do not understand the game. We ask the playing agent which choice pays more, and we seat it beside fixed partners, against whom the \emph{best response}, the play that earns the most, can be computed exactly.

\subsection{Asking the agent}

In the in-game question games we question the first seat at the start, in the middle and at the end of the game, about the state it has just decided on. Each question is a separate call, and its answer never enters the game. Rule questions only ask for numbers printed in the prompt, and all are answered correctly. We therefore rest on one value question, asked word for word:
\begin{quote}\small
\emph{Compare two strategies for the whole game. (A) You contribute 2 in every one of the 10 rounds and the group reaches the target, so you finish with 20 for certain. (B) You contribute 0 in every round and the group does not reach the target, so you keep 40 unless the disaster strikes. Which gives the higher AVERAGE final cash? Answer 1 if A is higher, 2 if B is higher, 0 if they are exactly equal.}
\end{quote}
The correct answer is B below $\popt$, A above it, and equal at $\popt$. A second value question could be read in two ways, so we report it only in the supplementary material.

\paragraph{Answers move with the risk; play does not.}
Claude Haiku 4.5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite answer the value question correctly almost every time, and GPT-5.6 Luna does so most of the time. All three change their answer with the risk (Figure~\ref{fig:knowdo}a): at low risk they almost always say that keeping pays more, and at high risk they mostly say the opposite, following the correct step. Their payments do not follow (Figure~\ref{fig:knowdo}b): the same seats pay the same amount at low and at high risk. Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20 show much less of this knowledge. Qwen3-235B always says that keeping pays more, whatever the risk, and Grok 4.20 gives that answer in the first round whatever the risk. The question lays out both strategies side by side, which is easier than finding the comparison inside the game, so the result is a gap between answers and play, not evidence about what an agent computes when it decides. A strongly risk-averse agent could also give these answers and still pay, so the gap rules out misreading the expected values but not such a preference; only payment at $p=0$ rules out both.

\subsection{Playing against fixed partners}

Next we seat one model beside five scripted partners of one kind. They always pay 0, always pay 2, always pay 4, or copy the group, which means paying 2 in the first round and then the rounded average of what the other seats paid in the previous round. The model is not told that its partners are scripted, but their moves are in the history. We found the best response to each kind by trying every possible sequence of the model's own moves. Beside partners who always pay 0 the target cannot be reached, and beside partners who always pay 4 it is reached without the model, so the best response to both is to pay nothing; once the history settles the outcome, paying is also dominated. Beside fair-share partners the best response is to pay nothing below $\popt$ and exactly the fair share above it, and partners who copy the group never move away from 2 and give the same answer.

\paragraph{No model keeps all its money where that is the best response.}
In no game beside partners who always pay 0 or always pay 4 does the model pay nothing. Only \ScrOptimalGames{} of the \ScrGames{} scripted games are exactly optimal, and every one of them is a game in which the optimum is the fair share. The models land on the optimum only where the optimum is the default. An agent that does not know its partners are scripted may reasonably pay early to learn who they are, so the cleaner evidence is payment after the outcome is settled (Table~\ref{tab:settled}).

\paragraph{Partners matter; risk does not.}
We split the variation in what a model pays beside the three fixed-amount partners into parts due to the partner, the risk and their interaction. For Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna and Qwen3-235B, the kind of partner explains most of the variation (\ScrOpponentShareMin\% to \ScrOpponentShareMax\%) and the risk with its interaction at most \ScrRiskShareMax\%, while for an exact best responder in the same games the risk would explain half of it. Beside fair-share partners, where the best response jumps from nothing to the fair share at $\popt$, no model's total moves by more than a single payment of 2 between low and high risk. Grok 4.20's total follows neither: it is set mostly by its opening move, made before any history exists, which is the largest payment in about half of its games and which it mostly repeats.

\paragraph{Responses to partners take simple forms.}
The models respond to their partners, but not the way a best responder would (Table~\ref{tab:settled}; the supplementary material shows every round). Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite pays its usual rate, and it and Claude Haiku 4.5 stop once the history shows that the outcome is settled. GPT-5.6 Luna pays little even before that point beside partners who always pay 4, which comes close to a best response while it learns who its partners are. Qwen3-235B pays more, not less, beside partners who pay nothing. Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20 keep paying large amounts after the outcome is settled, which no belief about the partners can justify. Qwen3-235B's last-round rule also costs its partners. Beside five fair-share partners it pays 2 for nine rounds and then nothing, so the table misses the target in almost every game, each time by that single missing payment. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite in the same seat reaches the target every time.

\paragraph{An exact fair share has no slack.}
One partner that stops paying in the last round is enough to break a table of exact fair-share players (Figure~\ref{fig:mixed}a). When the pool is one round of fair shares short of the target before the last round, Qwen3-235B pays nothing in \MixQwenPotZeroPct\% of cases and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite in \MixFlashPotZeroPct\%. Tables of Claude Haiku 4.5, which pays a little more than needed, or of Grok 4.20, which pays far more, absorb the same partner. Almost every missed table with a Qwen3-235B seat would have reached the target had that seat paid 2 in the last round. Slack also works the other way: one Grok 4.20 seat lets five Qwen3-235B seats reach the target in most games, and Grok 4.20 pays for the rescue. Since Qwen3-235B's last-round rule depends on the wording, this measures what one late dropout does to a group, whichever model plays that role.

\paragraph{Generous partners are free-ridden on.}
Most agents pay less, not more, when generous partners fill most of the table (Figure~\ref{fig:mixed}b), which moves them toward the best response, since five Grok 4.20 seats alone pass the target. Next to five Grok 4.20 seats, GPT-5.6 Luna pays about half of what it pays next to other partners, and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Claude Haiku 4.5 also fall below the fair share. As with scripted partners, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Claude Haiku 4.5 stop once the pool reaches the target, while GPT-5.6 Luna pays less from the start, so the generous partner carries the group. Qwen3-235B pays slightly more beside Grok 4.20. Grok 4.20's opening move, made before any history and varying across repetitions of a cell, separates watching the pool from free-riding: after a higher opening, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Claude Haiku 4.5 pay less in total mainly because the target is reached sooner, with little change while the pool is short, whereas GPT-5.6 Luna pays less while the pool is still short, the compensation theory predicts for adaptive players beside artificial agents~\cite{terrucha2024art}.

\section{Which agent wins under selection}
\label{sec:selection}

Systems that host many agents keep some and drop others: an operator picks the agent that earned most, or users copy the one that did well. What matters is whom an agent is compared with, and we show that this choice decides which of our five models such a process favours. Following empirical game-theoretic analysis~\cite{wellman2006methods,tuyls2018generalised}, we treat each model as a strategy. We write $\pi_A(k)$ for the mean expected payoff of an $A$ seat at a table with $k$ seats of $A$ and $6-k$ seats of another model $B$, estimated from the mixed tables for $1\le k\le5$ and from self-play for $k=6$.

\paragraph{Selection process.}
A population of $N$ agents holds $i$ agents of type $A$ and $N-i$ of type $B$, and each agent's five tablemates are drawn at random from the other $N-1$, as in~\cite{santos2011risk}. The fitness of each type is its payoff averaged over the tables it can land in:
\begin{align}
f_A(i) &= \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{6} \mathcal{H}(k-1;\,N-1,\,i-1)\;\pi_A(k), \label{eq:fitness}\\
f_B(i) &= \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{5} \mathcal{H}(k;\,N-1,\,i)\;\pi_B(6-k), \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{H}(k;\,N-1,\,m)$ is the hypergeometric chance that $k$ of the five other seats come from the $m$ other agents of type $A$. An agent copies a random other agent with probability $1/(1+e^{-\beta\Delta f})$, where $\Delta f$ is the other agent's fitness minus its own and the selection intensity $\beta$ is 1 per payoff unit. $\alpha$-Rank~\cite{omidshafiei2019alpha} assumes that a new model enters rarely, so the population almost always consists of one model. It computes the chance that a single newcomer takes over, for every pair of models, and turns these chances into a Markov chain over the five one-model populations. The share of time the chain spends on a model is the weight selection gives it, its \emph{stationary mass}. The population size sets whom an agent is compared with. With $N=6$ the whole population is one table, so an agent is compared only with its tablemates. With $N=30$, fitness is averaged over random tables, so an agent is compared mostly with agents who sat elsewhere.

\paragraph{Scoring against tablemates rewards the smallest payer.}
Within one game, Proposition~\ref{prop:table} makes the ranking mechanical: the seat that pays less earns more. What the data add is who pays least in company, which is Qwen3-235B, and what this selection costs the group. Selection gives Qwen3-235B the largest weight at every risk level (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}a). At $p=0.5$, where reaching the target is one of two optima, and at $p=0.9$, where it is the only one, the selected population reaches it far less often than a model picked at random (\SelWithinReachHigh\% against \SelUniformReachHigh\% at $p=0.9$), and stronger selection pushes group success close to zero. One game shows the mechanism. Beside five exact fair-share seats at $p=0.9$, a seat that skips its last payment makes the table miss, so its expected payoff falls from 20 to 2.2, yet it still earns more than each tablemate, who expects 2. This is the known result that selection on payoff relative to a small group favours whoever does better than its neighbours, even at a cost to the group~\cite{schaffer1988evolutionarily}, here shown with the play of deployed language models.

\paragraph{Scoring across tables keeps the fair-share player.}
When agents are compared with agents at other tables, what matters is how well a model's own tables do. At moderate and high risk, selection then favours Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}b), whose population always reaches the target and earns the optimum. It wins because its exact fair share is an equilibrium of Proposition~\ref{prop:eq} that it never leaves: a newcomer that pays less pushes its table below the target, and one that pays more loses money. The table-composition panels make the mechanism direct (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}c,\,d): in a mixed table, the lower payer can keep more of its endowment even when the group misses the target. At low risk both rules favour Qwen3-235B, which still earns far less than the optimum, since every model pays at low risk. The switch is sharp: the across-table winner already takes over in populations of seven or eight agents, so the smallest payer's advantage needs a population that is essentially one table. These winners do not change with stronger selection, with larger populations, or when self-play payoffs come from the risk grid alone, and each stays on top in at least \SelBootMinPct\% of bootstrap samples. Under weak selection no model gains much weight within a table, so the cost of tablemate scoring needs selection that is at least moderately strong. The same agents, scored in two reasonable ways, lead to opposite outcomes for the group, in line with finite-population theory, where the advantage of beating one's neighbours fades as the population grows~\cite{schaffer1988evolutionarily}. Which model plays each role rests on base-prompt signatures that change with wording (Section~\ref{sec:risk}), so the claim is the structure, not the name of the winner, and tablemate scoring is one simple route to the selfish outcomes that imitation produced for model-written strategies~\cite{willis2026evaluating}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Possible causes.}
Our data record choices, not reasoning, so we offer hypotheses. The equal split may be a focal point that models reconstruct without the hint~\cite{dodivers2025uncovering}; a question may call up computation while a move request calls up a default~\cite{fan2024can,schmied2026llms}; and post-training may favour doing one's share when nothing says otherwise, consistent with models being more cooperative than people~\cite{mei2024turing}. Stronger reasoning is not an obvious fix, since reasoning models can free-ride more in public goods games~\cite{guzmanpiedrahita2025corrupted}.

\paragraph{Consequences for design.}
State the agent's goal, since naming it removed most zero-risk payment. Report $\mathrm{opt}(p)$, a dominated-action condition and payment after the outcome is settled, since success can coexist with wasted money. Test in mixed populations, where an exact fair share breaks beside one late dropout. And compare agents across independent groups rather than with their tablemates.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
The goal sentence is itself an instruction, tested in one phrasing and in self-play only. The final-line format and a recalled fair share may raise the common payment level, but shared prompts cannot explain model differences.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
We test five low-cost models, one per provider, in English and in one game; more capable models may behave differently, so claims are about this tier. Ten games per cell limit power for the noisiest model. Rewordings test only a small prompt space, welfare losses assume risk neutrality, and scripted, mixed and selection tests use the base prompt. Mixed tables use fixed seat blocks, and $\alpha$-Rank assumes rare entry. We will release prompts, configurations, seeds and code with the final version; they reproduce the design, not the model transcripts.

\paragraph{Ethics.}
The study involves no human subjects and no personal data. We report failure modes of deployed systems so that designers can test for them before trusting agents with shared resources.
