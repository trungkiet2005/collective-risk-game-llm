\title{Best-responding to whom? Risk attitudes and opponent-conditioning of LLM agents in
collective-risk dilemmas}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affiliation{\institution{Anonymous Institution}\country{}}

\begin{abstract}
Language-model agents are being deployed into settings where their choices impose risk on
a group, yet most of the evidence about how they behave there comes from self-play, in
which one model faces copies of itself. Self-play cannot separate an agent reasoning about
its opponents from an agent executing a fixed script, because the two produce the same
transcript. We run $\PanelNgames$ games of the collective-risk social dilemma across
\PanelNmodelsWord{} production models and break that symmetry three ways. On an
\PanelNrisksWord{}-point grid of
catastrophe risk, \PanelNFlatWord{} of \PanelNmodelsWord{} models contribute the same
amount at every level, and a fourth changes only at the control at $p=0$. There
contributing is strictly dominated, yet $\PanelZeroSeatsPaying$ of $\PanelZeroSeats$ seats
still pay, and the panel forfeits $\PanelForfeitPanelPct\%$ of the payoff attainable at
the welfare-optimal outcome. Deleting the prompt's equal-split gloss
never lowers contribution and raises it to the endowment cap in two models, so the
cooperation is not compliance with a supplied focal point. Nor is it a failure of
comprehension: asked in the middle of their own games, the agents answer
$\PanelProbeRulesN$ questions about the rules at $\PanelProbeRulesAcc\%$ and identify the
higher-paying strategy at $p=\PanelProbeLowRisk$ at $\PanelProbeCompareLowAcc\%$, then
contribute $\PanelProbeLowContrib$ units against their own stated answer. Finally, seating
one model against $\EthreeaxSeatsScripted$ scripted opponents makes the best response computable: no model
plays it in any of $\EthreeaNgames$ games, including $\EthreeaDominatedGames$ in which
every alternative is strictly dominated, and a trivial free-rider out-earns every model in
the panel. Cooperation from these agents is a fixed schedule rather than a choice, which
makes them safe collaborators and unsafe stakeholders.
\end{abstract}
