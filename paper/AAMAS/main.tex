\begin{abstract}
% Language-model agents are increasingly deployed to act on behalf of people in settings where multiple agents’ decisions jointly determine a shared outcome. In many such settings, avoiding a collective loss requires each agent to cooperative, giving up some of its own resources. We ask whether such agents adjust their cooperation to the stakes and their partners' behaviours, or instead follow a largely fixed default strategy. We study five production models playing a collective-risk social dilemma game (CRG) in which six players contribute into a shared pool over ten rounds; if the pool misses its target, a catastrophe destroys all remaining savings with a given probability. Four models contribute about an equal share at every positive risk level, and four of the five also contribute at zero risk, where contributing  cannot help. Explicitly stating that only the agent's own  payoff matters reduces  contribution at zero risk for most models; simply making the prompt more neutral does not. Several models correctly identify which choice gives them the higher expected payoff at each risk level, yet barely change what they actually pay. Against fixed partners, no model consistently chooses the payoff-maximising best response, and in groups mixing different models, one agent that underpays in the final round can make an otherwise successful group fail. In payoff-based evolutionary selection, population size changes which model is favoured: with only one six-agent group, selection favours the model that contributes  least and reduces group success, whereas in larger randomly mixed populations it favours an exact fair-share model at moderate and high risk.
Language-model agents are increasingly deployed to act on behalf of people in settings where individual decisions shape shared outcomes. We examine whether five production models adapt their contributions to changing risks and partner behaviour, or instead follow a relatively fixed strategy. In a collective-risk dilemma game (CRP), six players contribute to a shared pool over ten rounds; if the pool falls short of its target, a catastrophe may destroy their remaining resources. Four models contributed approximately an equal share at every positive risk level, and four of the five also contributed when risk was zero, even though contributing could not improve their payoff. Explicitly stating that only the agent’s own payoff mattered reduced zero-risk contributions for most models, while a more neutral prompt did not. Several models correctly identified which action offered the higher expected payoff at different risk levels, yet their contributions changed little. Against fixed partners, no model consistently chose the payoff-maximising response, and in mixed-model groups, a single agent’s shortfall in the final round could cause an otherwise successful group to fail. Payoff-based selection also depended on population composition: in a single six-agent group, it favoured the model that contributed least and reduced group success, whereas in larger, randomly mixed populations, it favoured an exact fair-share model at moderate and high risk. These findings show that cooperative self-play alone is not evidence of strategic adaptability. Consistent with a machine-behaviour approach, evaluations of agents in multi-agent systems should test how they respond to incentives, partner actions, and population composition.
\end{abstract}

Language-model agents increasingly act on behalf of people in settings where multiple agents jointly shape a shared outcome, such as budget allocation, shared compute use, or negotiations over common resources\cite{chan2024visibility,hammond2025multi}. These systems are therefore not only engineering artifacts: their behaviour in interactive settings is also an object of empirical study, as emphasized by the emerging field of machine behaviour \cite{rahwan2019machine}. In many such settings, avoiding collective loss requires agents to incur individual costs for the benefit of the group~\cite{santos2011risk,santos2019outcome,milinski2008collective}. This raises a central question for multi-agent systems (MAS) research: do language-model agents condition their cooperative behaviour on the strategic environment, or do they rely on relatively fixed behavioural defaults? From a system-design and machine-behaviour perspective, this question has three components. First, is an agent’s cooperation sensitive to the magnitude of collective risk? Second, does it respond to the behaviour of other agents in the group? Third, under selection pressures that retain or replicate higher-earning agents, which behavioural tendencies are favoured?

Success in self-play, a common criterion in MAS benchmarks~\cite{piatti2024cooperate,huang2025competing}, does not by itself answer these questions. When all positions in a group are occupied by copies of the same model, an agent that conditions its decisions on the stakes may generate the same observed pattern of play as an agent that follows a fixed rule. A machine-behaviour approach therefore calls for testing agents across systematically varied environments, rather than inferring their decision policies from a single setting. Success rates likewise obscure the extent to which a group falls short of the attainable optimum: a group may achieve the collective target while still dissipating a substantial share of its endowment. Moreover, a prompt that specifies both a group target and a fixed number of rounds may implicitly suggest an equal division of the burden, causing agents that simply reproduce this split to appear cooperative under conventional summary measures.

We study these questions using the collective-risk social dilemma game (CRG), a game introduced to study collective decision making for climate change mitigation~\cite{milinski2008collective}. Players contribute into a shared pool over several rounds, and if the pool misses a target, they all lose their savings with probability $p$ (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a). For risk-neutral players, group welfare is maximised when no one contributes if $p < \tfrac{1}{2}$, and when the group meets the target exactly if $p > \tfrac{1}{2}$, for example through the \emph{fair share}, defined as an equal split of the target. The game also contains two situations in which contributing  cannot raise a player's own payoff: when there is no risk, and once the outcome is settled. Neither risk attitude nor concern for the group can explain  contribution in these situations, so they separate a default from a response to the stakes.

We introduce a game-theoretic evaluation that separates cooperative outcomes from incentive-sensitive behaviour by combining risk variation, dominated-action tests, exact best responses to fixed partners and selection in mixed populations, where each model is a strategy of an empirical game~\cite{wellman2006methods,tuyls2018generalised}. We apply it to five production models, Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna, Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20, in four designs (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b), each compared with a benchmark computed from the game. We use standard equilibrium and finite-population tools, not a new solution concept. We call near-flat  contribution across risk levels under a fixed prompt a \emph{default}; the term describes behaviour, not internal reasoning.

We report three sets of findings, one per question. (1)~\emph{Stakes.} Most models contribute roughly the fair share at every positive risk level, even when there is no risk. Removing normative wording does not change this pattern. Stating that only the agent’s own payoff matters reduces most zero-risk contributions, but only GPT-5.6 Luna shifts from contributing little at low risk to contributing the fair share at high risk. Asked in separate calls, several models rank the two choices correctly and change that ranking with the risk, but they keep contributing  the same amount (Section~\ref{sec:risk}). (2)~\emph{Partners.} Beside scripted partners who make every  contribution a loss, no model plays the best response of contributing  nothing, and two keep contributing  after the outcome is already settled. In a mixed game (also referred to as a table or group), a single partner that stops contributing in the last round often causes a table of exact fair-share players to fail, while a table that overpays can absorb the shortfall. (Section~\ref{sec:partners}). (3)~\emph{Selection.} Population size changes who is selected under payoff-based imitation: a population of one table favours the smallest  contributor  and lowers group success, whereas a larger randomly mixed population favours the exact fair-share player at moderate and high risk (Section~\ref{sec:selection}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Collective risk and selection.}
The collective-risk dilemma was introduced to study why groups fail to avoid a shared disaster, and human groups reach the target mostly when the risk is high~\cite{milinski2008collective}; inequality and communication change how often they succeed~\cite{tavoni2011inequality}. Threshold public goods games have many equilibria~\cite{palfrey1984participation}, contributions decline over the rounds of repeated public goods games~\cite{andreoni1988free}, and the risk of collective failure can turn a defection trap into a coordination problem~\cite{santos2011risk,merhej2021cooperation}. In evolutionary simulations of the same game, constant fair sharers lose out to players who hold back early and compensate later~\cite{abouchakra2012evolutionary}. Selection on payoff relative to a small group favours whoever does better than its neighbours~\cite{schaffer1988evolutionarily}, while selection between groups can favour cooperation~\cite{traulsen2006evolution}. Finite-population analyses price the incentives that sustain cooperation in this game~\cite{duong2026cost} and show that maximising cooperation need not maximise welfare~\cite{han2024evolutionary}. This literature predicts that behaviour should vary with risk, so the absence of such variation is informative; we use  a similar game theoretical approach to analyse how different agent types perform when placed together in mixed groups/tables.

\paragraph{Language models as strategic players.}
Language models have been studied as economic subjects~\cite{horton2023large,chen2023emergence,mei2024turing} and in repeated two-player games~\cite{akata2025playing,fontana2024nicer,pal2026large}. Models tend to be more cooperative than people~\cite{mei2024turing}, and an even split can reflect a focal point rather than fairness~\cite{dodivers2025uncovering}. The weight of framing relative to game structure differs by model~\cite{lore2024strategic}, and models can hold a correct belief or plan and act against it~\cite{fan2024can,schmied2026llms}. FAIRGAME runs language-model agents through standard games and finds outcomes that depend on model, language and persona~\cite{buscemi2025fairgame}; later work extends it to a payoff-scaled prisoner's dilemma and a multi-player public goods game~\cite{huynh2025understanding}.

\paragraph{Language models in social dilemmas.}
Societies of agents over-use a common resource~\cite{piatti2024cooperate}, contributions to a public good can stay the same even when it returns nothing~\cite{huang2025competing}, and reasoning models free-ride~\cite{guzmanpiedrahita2025corrupted}. Agents stand in for people in a collective-risk game~\cite{slumbers2025using} and for developers, regulators and users in an evolutionary model of AI regulation~\cite{buscemi2025llms}. A study of 35 open models includes a collective-risk game at one risk level and observes their decisions far from the group optimum~\cite{kumar2026cooperative}. In a binary collective-risk game with strategies written by three models, payoff-biased imitation drives larger groups to selfish, low-welfare outcomes~\cite{willis2026evaluating}, and prompts that evolve under individual selection collapse into defection while group selection keeps them prosocial~\cite{celiktemel2026group}. A recent study audits rule recall, state tracking and payoff calculation before interpreting play in multi-player races to develop AI~\cite{pham2026humans}. It compares play with an evolutionary benchmark and human data, and finds that correct recall can coexist with weak expected-payoff calculation. We differ in three ways: we vary the risk from zero to certainty and add two tests in which contributing  cannot raise the  contributor 's cash; we compute the exact best response to fixed partners; and we select among deployed models with payoffs from their real mixed tables, finding that at moderate and high risk the favoured model changes once the population exceeds one table.

\paragraph{Multiagent evaluation.}
Multiagent reinforcement learning studies cooperation in sequential social dilemmas~\cite{leibo2017multi,hughes2018inequity}, and ad hoc teamwork and cooperative AI ask for agents that work with partners unlike themselves~\cite{stone2010ad,dafoe2020open,leibo2021scalable}. Empirical game-theoretic analysis treats trained agents as strategies of a meta-game~\cite{wellman2006methods,tuyls2018generalised}, $\alpha$-Rank ranks them by evolutionary dynamics~\cite{omidshafiei2019alpha}, and evolutionary game theory is used to study emergent behaviour in multiagent systems~\cite{han2022emergent}. Language models show distinct strategic fingerprints in tournaments with classic strategies~\cite{payne2025strategic}, their cooperation evolves differently across base models~\cite{vallinder2025cultural}, and cooperation sustained by repetition weakens when co-players differ~\cite{tewolde2026coopeval}. In the collective-risk game, people who delegate to artificial agents succeed more often~\cite{fernandezdomingos2022delegation}, and in theory adaptive players compensate for the effort of artificial agents~\cite{terrucha2024art}. We add a multiplayer dilemma with exact best responses to fixed partners, and measure this compensation in real models.


\section{Game and evaluation design}
\label{sec:game}

\subsection{Collective-risk game and benchmarks}
\label{sec:rules}
Six players each start with $e=40$ units, and the game lasts $R=10$ rounds. In each round every player privately chooses a contribution $a_i^t\in\{0,2,4\}$ to a shared \emph{pool}, and after each round all players see what every seat has contributed. Let $x_i=\sum_t a_i^t$ be a player's total and $X=\sum_i x_i$ the pool. The target is $T=120$. A player keeps what it did not contribute, but if the pool misses the target, a catastrophe destroys all savings with probability $p$ (Table~\ref{tab:terms} lists the terms used):
\begin{equation}
u_i \;=\;
\begin{cases}
e-x_i & \text{if } X\ge T,\\
0 \text{ with probability } p, \text{ else } e-x_i & \text{if } X<T.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:payoff}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:eq}
Consider the expected-payoff formulation of the game in which each player picks a total $x_i\in\{0,2,\dots,40\}$, and let $p<1$. A profile is a Nash equilibrium if and only if $x=0$, or $X=T$ and $x_i\le p\times e$ for every $i$. A target-reaching equilibrium exists if and only if $p\ge\popt=T/(6e)=\tfrac12$. The welfare-optimal outcome is $x=0$ for $p<\popt$ and $X=T$ for $p>\popt$, and both are optimal at $p=\popt$, so the welfare-optimal mean payoff per player is $\mathrm{opt}(p)=\max\{(1-p)e,\;e-T/6\}$; this welfare statement also holds at $p=1$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Proof sketch.} If $X<T$, any player with $x_i>0$ gains by contributing  nothing, so the only equilibrium that misses the target is $x=0$. If $X>T$, then $X\ge T+2$ because totals are even, so a player can contribute 2 less and still reach the target. If $X=T$, the best deviation is to contribute nothing, worth $(1-p)e$ against $e-x_i$, which gives $x_i\le pe$; summing over players gives $p\ge T/(6e)$, and conversely $x_i=20$ for every $i$ is an equilibrium when $p\ge\tfrac12$. Welfare is $6e-X$ when the target is met and $(1-p)(6e-X)$ otherwise, so it peaks at $X=T$ or $X=0$. The ten-round game, in which strategies can react to the history, has the same totals in pure-strategy Nash equilibrium; the Supplementary Material gives the full proof (Proposition~S1, Section~S2.3). We claim nothing about subgame perfection, and $\mathrm{opt}(p)$ is a welfare bound that does not depend on the equilibrium concept.\hfill$\square$

\paragraph{What we compare play with.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:eq} gives a \emph{symmetric welfare benchmark}: keep everything below the pivot and contribute the fair share above it. Asymmetric allocations reaching exactly $T$ attain the same group welfare. This benchmark is not a unique equilibrium or a prediction of individual play: contributing  nothing is also an equilibrium at every risk level. Contributing  is dominated in own  payoff at $p=0$ and, for $p<1$, after the outcome is settled. These tests do not require risk neutrality or beliefs about the partners. They do assume that larger own  payoff is preferred; non- payoff goals are not ruled out.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:table}
For two seats $i$ and $j$ in the same game, the difference in their expected payoffs over the lottery is $c\,(x_j-x_i)$, with $c=1$ if the target was reached and $c=1-p$ otherwise.
\end{proposition}

\noindent Both seats face the same pool and the same lottery, so only their own contributions differ. Hence, for $p<1$, whoever contributes  less at a table earns more, whatever the group achieves.

\subsection{Models, protocol and measures}
\label{sec:setup}

We test the five models of Section~\ref{sec:intro}: one production model per provider, chosen to reflect the kind of model plausibly used for deploying many agents at scale. Qwen3-235B is the instruction-tuned build without a thinking mode, and Grok 4.20 is its non-reasoning variant.

\paragraph{Prompts.}
In each round a seat receives a prompt with the rules, the full history of every seat's contributions, and a request for making a contribution; the Supplementary Material provides every prompt word for word (Section~S4). The base prompt calls the pool a ``climate account'', says that the group ``must'' reach the target, calls the loss a ``disaster'' and names the game as a collective-risk social dilemma. It never states the player's goal, and it hints at the equal split twice. The prompt controls in Section~\ref{sec:designs} change exactly these parts.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
The running pool is not shown,  so any seat that needs it must compute it from the history. The prompt is in English and no seat is assigned a persona. We parse the move from the reply's final \texttt{CONTRIBUTION:} line.

\paragraph{Measures.}
We report \emph{expected} payoffs, the average a seat would earn if the catastrophe lottery were run many times on the same contribution, and the share of $\mathrm{opt}(p)$ that is lost. Proposition~\ref{prop:eq}, $\mathrm{opt}(p)$ and the selection fitness below all use expected payoffs, which remove the lottery noise without bias; agents never see the draw, and the Supplementary Material lists the realised draws (Table~S6). The unit of analysis is the game, since seats in one game share a history and a lottery. Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstraps over games and tests are permutation tests over games;  Supplementary Material gives every value behind the statements below (Sections~S6--S12).

\paragraph{Self-play and prompt controls.}
Six copies of one model play the base prompt at 11 risk levels  $p=0, 0.1, \dots,1$, and again under prompt and decoding controls at fewer risk levels. \emph{No hint} deletes the two equal-split phrases. \emph{Neutral wording} replaces the normative and naming phrases with plain ones, such as ``a shared account''. \emph{Goal stated} keeps the neutral wording and adds an opening that says the player is in a paid experiment where ``the only thing that matters to you is your own final cash payoff''. Two \emph{rewordings} change the phrasing but keep every rule and the hint, one condition sets the temperature to zero, and a \emph{rerun} repeats the base prompt with value questions.

\paragraph{Value questions.}
In the rerun, separate calls whose answers never enter the game ask the first seat at the start, middle and end of the game which of two whole-game strategies gives the higher average payoff: contributing 2 in every round and reaching the target, or contributing nothing and missing it. The correct answer switches at $\popt$ (Supplementary Material, Section~S8).

\paragraph{Fixed partners.}
One model plays with five scripted partners of the same type. They always contribute 0, always contribute 2, always contribute 4, or copy the group, which means contributing  2 in the first round and then the allowed amount nearest to the average of what the other seats contributed in the previous round. The model is not informed that its partners are scripted, but their moves are included in the history. We compute the \emph{best response} to each type, i.e. the play that earns the most, by enumerating every possible sequence of the model’s own ten moves. With partners who always contribute 0, the target cannot be reached; with partners who always contribute 4, the target is reached without the model. The best response to both is to contribute nothing, and once the history settles the outcome, contributing cannot improve the outcome and only reduces payoff. Against fair-share partners, the best response is to contribute nothing below $\popt$ and exactly the fair share above it; partners who copy the group never deviate from 2, thus resulting in the same best response.

\paragraph{Mixed tables.}
To mix defaults at a table, we seat every pair of models together, with one to five seats for the first model and the rest for the second, at low, moderate and high risk. Each model's seats form one block, fixed within a cell and alternating across cells, so every model occupies every seat equally often  overall.

\paragraph{Selection among models.}
We finally examine which model prevails under payoff-based retention or imitation, and how this outcome depends on population size. We treat each LLM as a strategy in an empirical game, i.e. using experimentally estimated payoffs from model interactions to define the game~\cite{wellman2006methods,tuyls2018generalised}. We write $\pi_A(k)$ for the mean expected payoff of an $A$ seat at a table with $k$ seats of $A$ and $6-k$ of $B$, from mixed tables for $1\le k\le5$ and from self-play (grid plus rerun) for $k=6$. A population of $N$ agents consists of $i$ agents of type $A$ and $N-i$ of type $B$, and each agent's five tablemates are drawn at random from the other $N-1$, as in e.g. ~\cite{santos2011risk,han2017evolution,santos2020picky}. The fitness of each type is its payoff averaged over the tables/interactions it participates in:
\begin{align}
f_A(i) &= \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{6} \mathcal{H}(k-1;\,N-1,\,i-1)\;\pi_A(k), \label{eq:fitness}\\
f_B(i) &= \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{5} \mathcal{H}(k;\,N-1,\,i)\;\pi_B(6-k), \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{H}(k;\,N-1,\,m)$ is the hypergeometric chance that $k$ of five tablemates come from the $m$ other agents of type $A$. Under Fermi imitation~\cite{traulsen2006stochastic,han2017evolution}, an randomly selected agent copies a random other agent's strategy with probability $1/(1+e^{-\beta\Delta f})$, where $\Delta f$ is the other's fitness minus its own and $\beta=1$ per payoff unit. We assume rare mutation~\cite{fudenberg2006imitation,han2017evolution}, which has been shown to provide a strong approximation beyond the strict rare-mutation regime~\cite{han2012emergence,rand2013evolution,zisis2015generosity,sigmund2010social}: each newcomer either fixes or disappears before the next arrives. Pairwise fixation probabilities then define a Markov chain over the five homogeneous model populations.  Its \emph{stationary mass} gives each model's long-run weight, and EGTtools~\cite{fernandezdomingos2023egttools} was used to reproduce the fixation probabilities and stationary distributions of Figure~\ref{fig:selection}. With $N=6$ the whole population is one table; with $N=30$, fitness averages over random, overlapping tables. Changing $N$ changes both table compositions and fixation dynamics, not only whom an agent is compared with. We evaluate this finite-population process, not a scoring-only intervention.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Stakes:  contribution does not scale with risk}
\label{sec:risk}

\subsubsection{Self-play across risk}
\label{sec:grid}

Four of the five models contribute approximately  the fair share at every positive risk level (Figure~\ref{fig:selfplay}a). Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna and Qwen3-235B contribute almost the same amount below the pivot as above it (a step of at most \GridStepMaxOther{} units), whereas the symmetric welfare benchmark jumps from contributing nothing to the fair share. In the original experiment with the same rules, human groups reached the target in half of their games at *p* = 0.9 and in none at *p* = 0.1~\cite{milinski2008collective}. Strong risk aversion could explain a flat contribution profile above zero risk: because catastrophe leaves players with nothing, a player with relative risk aversion near one may prefer a certain fair-share contribution even at low risk. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite provides the clearest example. Almost every seat plays the exact split at every risk level (Table~\ref{tab:models}), so this near-fixed rule attains the welfare optimum above the pivot but not below it.
\paragraph{Paying when there is nothing to protect.}
Four of the five models also contribute at $p=0$, about as much as at any other risk level (Table~\ref{tab:models}, first column). Contributing  there only lowers a player's own payoff and helps nobody, so neither risk attitude nor concern for others'  payoff can explain this contribution. GPT-5.6 Luna is the exception. It contributes  nothing at $p=0$ and its usual share at every positive risk, so it distinguishes zero risk from positive risk but does not respond to how high the risk is.

\paragraph{Three ways to lose.}
The models lose payoff in three different ways (Table~\ref{tab:models}, Figure~\ref{fig:selfplay}c). Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.6 Luna lose mostly at low risk, where they contribute for a target that is not worth reaching. Grok 4.20 overpays: its groups reach the target well before the last round, and its seats keep contributing  into a pool that is already safe. Qwen3-235B fails at the end. It contributes  2 round after round, then almost all of its seats contribute nothing in the last round (Figure~\ref{fig:selfplay}b). Its groups therefore almost never reach the target, although every one of them would have succeeded had each seat simply repeated its previous move. Success rates hide all of this. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Grok 4.20 both reach the target in every game, yet Grok 4.20 keeps far less of its endowment . For $0.5<p<1$, Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20 groups earn less in most games than in the worst equilibrium of Proposition~\ref{prop:eq}, in which nobody contributes. In the last round, Qwen3-235B skips as often when its contribution is needed as when the others' fair shares already suffice, so its skip follows the round number rather than the pool; human contributions similarly decline over the rounds of repeated public goods games~\cite{andreoni1988free}. GPT-5.6 Luna skips mostly when its contribution is not needed, and Claude Haiku 4.5 stops mostly once the target is reached (Supplementary Material, Section~S12.1).

\subsubsection{Prompt and objective controls}
\label{sec:controls}

\paragraph{The default effect persists under rewording, but the resulting failures do not.}
Deleting the two equal-split phrases does not reduce any model’s contribution (Table~\ref{tab:robust}), indicating that the hint was not the source of contributions. Instead, it bounded contributions for some models while enabling others contribute exactly 2 per round. Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite still plays the exact split, which it can rebuild from the target, while GPT-5.6 Luna keeps its average  contribution but plays the exact split less often. Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20 contribute more without the hint; Qwen3-235B now reaches the target, but it earns less because it overpays. The two rewordings and a temperature of zero leave the contributions of the four models other than Grok 4.20 almost unchanged. The failures are less stable than the averages. Under the second rewording Grok 4.20 contributes  about the fair share, and how often Qwen3-235B drops out in the last round varies widely across prompts. Grok 4.20's rise with risk on the grid recurs at temperature zero but not in the rerun or the second rewording, so we do not interpret it as a stable response.

\paragraph{Stating the goal, not removing norms, reduces  zero-risk contributions.}
The normative words are not the main source of the default. With neutral wording, most models still contribute at $p=0$. At low and high risk the four models other than Grok 4.20 contribute on average what they contributed before, while Grok 4.20 contributes  even more and GPT-5.6 Luna plays the exact split less often (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). Adding the stated goal is what changes play at zero risk: Claude Haiku 4.5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite then contribute almost nothing, Qwen3-235B contributes  far less than before, and Grok 4.20 barely changes. The goal still does not make most models weigh the risk. Claude Haiku 4.5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite now separate zero from positive risk but already contribute the fair share at low risk, a pattern that strong risk aversion could also produce. Only GPT-5.6 Luna changes from contributing  little at low risk to the fair share at high risk. Qwen3-235B contributes  slightly more at high than at low risk but less than before at every risk level, and never reaches the target; Grok 4.20 still overpays. 

For most models, behaviour at zero risk changes depending on whether the prompt clearly states the goal. When risk above zero, models tend to contribute  similar amounts across risk levels, and this pattern remains under different wordings. The partner and selection results below use the base prompt.

\paragraph{Correct answers, unchanged play.}
Asked the value question in separate calls, Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and GPT-5.6 Luna mostly answer correctly and change their answer with the risk, yet the questioned seats contribute almost the same at low and high risk; Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20 mostly answer that keeping pays more. This is a gap between answers and play, not evidence about what an agent computes; the Supplementary Material gives every answer and the answers against play (Section~S8.6). A strongly risk-averse agent could give these answers and still contribute; contribution at $p=0$ and after settlement provides the sharper test.

\subsection{Partners: simple responses and fragile tables}
\label{sec:partners}

\subsubsection{Fixed partners}
\label{sec:scripted}

\paragraph{No model keeps all its endowment  when that is the best response.}
The model contributes nothing only when paired with partners who always contribute either 0 or 4. Of the \ScrGames{} scripted games, only \ScrOptimalGames{} are played exactly optimally, and in every case the optimum is the fair share. In other words, the models reach the optimum only when it coincides with the default contribution. Since an agent unaware that its partners are scripted may reasonably contribute early to learn their behaviour, the cleaner evidence comes from contributions made after the outcome has settled (Table~\ref{tab:settled}).

\paragraph{Partners matter, risk does not.}
We split the variation in what a model contributes  across games with  three fixed-amount partners  into partner, risk and interaction parts. For Claude Haiku 4.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, GPT-5.6 Luna and Qwen3-235B, partner type  explains most of the variation (\ScrOpponentShareMin\% to \ScrOpponentShareMax\%), while risk and its interaction with partner type explain at most  \ScrRiskShareMax\%. For an exact best responder in the same games,  risk would explain half of  the variation. Beside fair-share partners, where the best response jumps from nothing to the fair share at $\popt$, no model’s total contribution changes by more than 2 between the low- and high-risk conditions. Grok 4.20's total follows neither pattern: it is driven largely by its opening move, which is its largest contribution in around half of its games.

\paragraph{Responses to partners take simple forms.}
The models adjust their contributions in response to their partners, but not in the way a best responder would (Table~\ref{tab:settled}; Figures~S4 and~S5 of the Supplementary Material show every round). Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite contributes  its usual amount, while both it and Claude Haiku 4.5 stop contributing once the history indicates that the outcome is settled. With partners who always contribute 4, GPT-5.6 Luna contributes  little even  the outcome is settled, remaining close to a best response as it learns its partners’ behaviour. Qwen3-235B contributes more, not less, when interacting with partners who contribute nothing. Both Qwen3-235B and Grok 4.20 also continue making large contributions after the outcome is settled -- a behaviour that no belief about their partners can justify. Against five fair-share partners, Qwen3-235B contributes  2 for nine rounds and then nothing, so the table misses the target in almost every game by that single payment; Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite in the same seat reaches it every time.

\paragraph{An exact fair-share target leaves no margin for error.}
One partner who stops contributing in the final round can cause a table of  fair-share players to fail (Figure~\ref{fig:mixed}a). When the pool is one round of fair shares short before the last round, Qwen3-235B contributes  nothing in \MixQwenPotZeroPct\% of cases and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite in \MixFlashPotZeroPct\%. Five Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite seats with one Qwen3-235B seat succeed in only \MixFlashQwenReach{} of 30 games, although Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite alone never misses in our sample; tables with GPT-5.6 Luna also succeed less often. Tables of Claude Haiku 4.5 or Grok 4.20, which contribute more, absorb the same partner. Replacing \emph{all} Qwen3-235B last-round zeros by 2, holding other recorded actions fixed, would rescue \MixQwenPivotalFix\% of the \MixMissWithQwen{} failed mixed tables containing it. is is an arithmetic counterfactual, not a tested intervention.  Conversely, one Grok 4.20 seat helps five Qwen3-235B seats succeed in most games, contributing  for the rescue. These comparisons depend on the base prompt and the fixed seat blocks.

\paragraph{Agents free-ride on generous partners.}
Most agents contribute less as the number of Grok 4.20 seats increases from one to five (Figure~\ref{fig:mixed}b). Alongside five Grok 4.20 seats,  GPT-5.6 Luna contributes  \MixLunaBesideFiveGrok{} units, and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Claude Haiku 4.5 also fall below the fair share; these two stop once the pool reaches the target, while GPT-5.6 Luna contributes  less from the start. Qwen3-235B does not show this decline. Within a cell, Grok 4.20's higher opening contributions are associated with lower total contributions by Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite and Claude Haiku 4.5, mainly through earlier target completion. GPT-5.6 Luna also contributes  less while the pool is short, consistent with the compensation predicted alongside artificial agents~\cite{terrucha2024art}. Openings are not randomised and partners can react to a deviation, so this association identifies neither a causal response nor an exact best response.


\subsection{Selection: population-size dependent }
\label{sec:selection}

% \paragraph{In a population of one table, selection rewards the smallest  contributor .}
% Within one game, Proposition~\ref{prop:table} makes the ranking mechanical: the seat that contributes  less earns more. What the data add is who contributes  least in company, which is Qwen3-235B, and what this selection costs the group. Selection gives Qwen3-235B the largest weight at every risk level (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}a). Reaching the target is one of two optima at $p=0.5$ and the only one at $p=0.9$. At both risks, the selected population reaches it far less often than a model picked at random (\SelWithinReachHigh\% against \SelUniformReachHigh\% at $p=0.9$), and stronger selection pushes group success close to zero. A worked example shows the mechanism. Beside five exact fair-share seats at $p=0.9$, a seat that skips its last  contribution makes the table miss, so its expected payoff falls from 20 to 2.2, yet it still earns more than each tablemate, who expects 2. This outcome is the known result that selection on payoff relative to a small group favours whoever does better than its neighbours, even at a cost to the group~\cite{schaffer1988evolutionarily}, here shown with deployed language models.
In a one-table population, selection favours the model that contributes least. Proposition~\ref{prop:table} explains why: within a game, contributing less leads to a higher individual payoff relative to the other players. In our data, Qwen3-235B contributes least relative to its tablemates, so selection gives it the greatest weight (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}a). But this individual advantage comes at a cost to the group: tables reach the target less often when models are selected this way than when a model is chosen uniformly at random. At $p=0.9$, for example, the target is reached in \SelWithinReachHigh\% of selected games, compared with \SelUniformReachHigh\% of randomly selected games. This illustrates the known result that selection based on payoffs relative to a small group favours whoever does better than its neighbours, even when this comes at a cost to the group~\cite{schaffer1988evolutionarily}. Here, we observe this dynamic in games played by deployed language models.

%\paragraph{A larger population favours the fair-share player.}
In  larger populations, selection favours Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite at moderate and high risk (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}b). Its self-play attains the welfare optimum there, but neither optimality nor Nash equilibrium implies that imitation will select it. The ranking comes from the estimated payoffs at every composition along the fixation path, where a lower  contributor  can earn more than its tablemates even while lowering its own payoff (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}c,\,d). At low risk both population sizes favour Qwen3-235B, whose outcome remains far below the optimum. The switch to Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite occurs already at seven or eight agents, consistent with finite-population theory~\cite{schaffer1988evolutionarily}. 
%Each winner stays on top in at least \SelBootMinPct\% of bootstrap samples, and the same winners appear at $\beta=\SelBetaExtra$, at $N$ of \SelNExtra{} and with grid-only self-play payoffs. Under weak selection ($\beta=\SelWeakBeta$), the largest one-table mass is only \SelWeakWithinMaxMass{} and GPT-5.6 Luna narrowly leads at moderate and high risk. \section{Conclusion and discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results show that cooperative-looking self-play is not sufficient evidence that an agent responds appropriately to incentives or adapts to its partners. Across the five models, contributions often remain close to the fair share as risk changes, including when contributing is payoff-dominated; some models also contribute after their actions can no longer affect the outcome. In mixed tables, one late shortfall can make an exact fair-share target fail. Selection outcomes depend on population structure: in a one-table population, selection can favour the lowest contributor at the group’s expense, while larger randomly mixed populations favour the fair-share model at moderate and high risk.

These findings matter for how we understand machine behaviour. A cooperative action need not reflect a stable preference for cooperation or an understanding of its consequences: it may instead be a default response to the prompt or a learned focal-point strategy. This empirical approach to auditing agent behaviour—treating models as subjects for observation in varied, interactive environments—aligns with the call to systematically study machine behaviour~\cite{rahwan2019machine}. Conversely, correctly identifying the payoff-maximising action when asked does not ensure that a model will choose it in play. We therefore distinguish what models can state from how they behave, and caution against treating self-play success as evidence of strategic competence, social preference, or reliable adaptation. Evaluations should vary incentives and partner behaviour, test mixed populations, and compare individual payoffs with group welfare. They should also report the welfare benchmark, identify dominated actions, and measure contributions after the outcome is settled. This can reveal wasted resources and cases where apparent cooperation does not improve welfare~\cite{han2024evolutionary}. 


The mechanisms behind these behaviours remain uncertain: our data show choices, not reasoning. One possibility is that an equal split acts as a focal point that models reconstruct even without an explicit hint~\cite{dodivers2025uncovering}. Another is that questions prompt calculation while requests to act elicit a behavioural default~\cite{fan2024can,schmied2026llms}. Post-training may also encourage models to contribute their share unless instructed otherwise, consistent with findings that models are more cooperative than people~\cite{mei2024turing} and that cooperation increases with the stakes in repeated prisoner’s dilemmas, contrary to evolutionary predictions~\cite{huynh2026payoff}. Stronger reasoning is not necessarily a remedy: reasoning models can free-ride more in public-goods games~\cite{guzmanpiedrahita2025corrupted}. These accounts are hypotheses, not explanations established by our experiments.

 \noindent\textbf{Threats, limitations and ethics.}
 The goal sentence is an instruction tested in one phrasing and in self-play only, and without a group-payoff goal we cannot separate objective clarity from the direction of the instruction. The running pool is not shown, so arithmetic errors may explain some  contribution after settlement, and the value questions omit $p=0$. We test five models, one per provider, in English and one game; claims concern this panel, not language models in general. Ten games per cell limit power, and a zero-width bootstrap interval in a constant sample does not prove deterministic behaviour. Rewordings cover a small prompt space, welfare losses assume risk neutrality, the scripted, mixed and selection designs use the base prompt, fixed seat blocks confound composition with position, and selection assumes rare entry. We will release prompts, configurations, seeds and code; they reproduce the design, not the model transcripts. The study involves no human subjects or personal data, and we report failure modes so designers can test for them before trusting agents with shared resources.
