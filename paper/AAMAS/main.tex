\title{Best-responding to whom? Risk attitudes and opponent-conditioning of LLM agents in
collective-risk dilemmas}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affiliation{\institution{Anonymous Institution}\country{}}

\begin{abstract}
Language-model agents are being deployed into settings where their choices impose risk on
a group, yet most of the evidence about how they behave there comes from self-play, in
which one model faces copies of itself. Self-play cannot separate an agent that reasons
about its opponents from an agent that runs a fixed schedule, because the two produce the
same transcript. We run $\PanelNgames$ games of the collective-risk social dilemma across
\PanelNmodelsWord{} production models and break that symmetry three ways.

Across an \PanelNrisksWord{}-point grid of catastrophe risk, the panel pays on a schedule
that the risk does not move. At the control where no catastrophe is possible, and
contributing is therefore strictly dominated under every increasing utility, most seats
still pay, and the panel forfeits $\PanelForfeitPanelPct\%$ of the payoff that was
attainable. Deleting the prompt's equal-split gloss never lowers contribution, so the
cooperation is not compliance with a supplied focal point. Nor is it misunderstanding:
asked about the rules in the middle of their own games, the agents name the higher-paying
action and then contribute against their own answer.

Seating one model against scripted opponents makes the best response computable, and no
model plays it in any game, including the games in which every alternative is strictly
dominated. Seating two different models at one table shows what is missing.
\ErecipNBlind{} of \EthreebModels{} act on the running total in the shared account rather
than on the agents filling it, and the one model whose response to its partners is large
enough to appear in play gives less to partners who give more.

Cooperation from these agents is a fixed schedule rather than a choice. That makes them
safe collaborators and unsafe stakeholders.
\end{abstract}
