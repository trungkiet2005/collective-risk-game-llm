\section{The game and its benchmarks}
\label{sec:game}

\subsection{Rules and notation}

Six players each start with an endowment $e=40$. In each of $R=10$ rounds, every player chooses privately and at the same time a contribution $a_i^t\in\{0,2,4\}$ to a shared pool, and after each round every seat's contribution is shown to all. A player's total is $x_i=\sum_{t=1}^{R}a_i^t\in\{0,2,\dots,40\}$ and the pool is $X=\sum_{i=1}^{6}x_i$. If $X\ge T=120$, every player keeps $e-x_i$. If $X<T$, a single lottery for the whole table destroys all savings with probability $p$, and otherwise every player keeps $e-x_i$. For a risk-neutral player the expected payoff is therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ev}
u_i(x)=\begin{cases} e-x_i & \text{if } X\ge T,\\ (1-p)\,(e-x_i) & \text{if } X<T.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The fair share is $T/6=20$ per player, or 2 per round, and the pivot is $\popt=T/(6e)=\tfrac12$. All results below assume $p<1$. At $p=1$ a missed target leaves every player with nothing whatever it paid, so many profiles that miss the target become weak equilibria and the characterisation changes.

\subsection{Proposition 1 and its proof}

\begin{proposition}
Consider the game in which each player picks a total $x_i\in\{0,2,\dots,40\}$, with payoffs \eqref{eq:ev} and $p<1$. A profile is a Nash equilibrium if and only if $x=0$, or $X=T$ and $x_i\le pe$ for every $i$. A target-reaching equilibrium exists if and only if $p\ge\popt$. The welfare-optimal outcome is $x=0$ for $p<\popt$ and $X=T$ for $p>\popt$, and both are optimal at $p=\popt$, so the best attainable payoff per player is $\mathrm{opt}(p)=\max\{(1-p)e,\;e-T/6\}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We check the three cases $X<T$, $X>T$ and $X=T$ in turn.

\emph{Case $X<T$.} Suppose some player has $x_i>0$. If it pays nothing instead, the pool is still below $T$ and its payoff rises from $(1-p)(e-x_i)$ to $(1-p)e$, which is strictly more because $p<1$. So a profile that misses the target can be an equilibrium only if $x=0$. Conversely, $x=0$ is an equilibrium: a single player can raise the pool to at most $40<T$, so any deviation $x_i'>0$ yields $(1-p)(e-x_i')<(1-p)e$.

\emph{Case $X>T$.} Totals are even and $T$ is even, so $X\ge T+2$. Some player has $x_i>0$ and can pay 2 less. The pool stays at or above $T$, and its payoff rises by 2. No such profile is an equilibrium.

\emph{Case $X=T$.} Paying more lowers a player's payoff, since the target is met anyway. Paying less makes the pool miss the target, and the best such deviation is to pay nothing, which yields $(1-p)e$. So player $i$ has no profitable deviation if and only if $e-x_i\ge(1-p)e$, that is $x_i\le pe$.

\emph{Existence.} If $p\ge\popt$, then $pe\ge20$ and the profile in which every player pays 20 is an equilibrium. If $p<\popt$, then $pe<20$, every equilibrium total is below 20, and six of them cannot add up to $T=120$.

\emph{Welfare.} Total welfare is $6e-X$ when $X\ge T$ and $(1-p)(6e-X)$ when $X<T$. Over profiles that reach the target it is largest at $X=T$, with value $6e-T$; over profiles that miss it, it is largest at $X=0$, with value $(1-p)\,6e$. The first is larger if and only if $p>T/(6e)=\popt$, and they are equal at $\popt$. Dividing by six gives $\mathrm{opt}(p)$.
\end{proof}

The statement is also checked by exhaustive search over every symmetric profile and several thousand random asymmetric profiles at eleven risk levels, as part of the released tests.

\subsection{The ten-round game}

In the ten-round game a pure strategy maps every history of past contributions to a contribution in $\{0,2,4\}$, and payoffs still depend only on the totals through \eqref{eq:ev}.

\begin{lemma}
\label{prop:repeated}
Let $p<1$. The totals $x$ reached on the path of a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium of the ten-round game are exactly the equilibrium profiles of Proposition~1. Each of them is reached by an equilibrium in which every player follows a fixed schedule that ignores the history.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Every profile of Proposition~1 is reached.} Let each player follow any fixed schedule of contributions in $\{0,2,4\}$ that adds up to $x_i$; one exists because $x_i\le40$. The other players' totals then do not depend on what player $i$ does, so whatever strategy $i$ uses, it ends with some total $x_i'$ against fixed totals of the others. Its payoff is the payoff of the one-shot totals game, which by Proposition~1 is at most the payoff of $x_i$. No deviation is profitable.

\emph{Only such profiles are reached.} Fix a pure equilibrium and its path totals $x$.

(i) The strategy ``always pay 0'' guarantees at least $(1-p)e$ against anything, since it earns $e$ if the target is met and $(1-p)e$ otherwise. In equilibrium every player therefore earns at least $(1-p)e$.

(ii) If $X<T$, a player earns $(1-p)(e-x_i)$, which is at least $(1-p)e$ only if $x_i=0$. So $x=0$.

(iii) If $X\ge T$, a player earns $e-x_i\ge(1-p)e$, so $x_i\le pe$.

(iv) It remains to rule out $X>T$. Let $\tau$ be the first round after which the path pool is at least $T$. If some player $i$ pays a positive amount in a round after $\tau$, let $t'$ be the last such round for that player, and let $i$ follow its strategy up to round $t'-1$ and pay 0 from round $t'$ on. The target is already met after round $\tau$ and contributions cannot be negative, so it stays met whatever the others do later, and $i$'s total falls by at least 2: a profitable deviation. Otherwise nobody pays after $\tau$, so the pool after round $\tau$ equals $X\ge T+2$. Some player $i$ pays $a_i^\tau\ge2$ in round $\tau$. Let $i$ pay $a_i^\tau-2$ in round $\tau$ and 0 afterwards. Decisions within a round are simultaneous, so the others' round-$\tau$ contributions are unchanged, the pool after round $\tau$ is $X-2\ge T$, and $i$ gains 2. Either way the profile is not an equilibrium, so $X=T$.

Together, (ii) to (iv) give exactly the conditions of Proposition~1.
\end{proof}

We claim nothing about subgame perfection: the fixed schedules above need not be optimal after histories that are off the path.

\subsection{Proposition 2 and its proof}

\begin{proposition}
For two seats $i$ and $j$ in the same game, $u_i-u_j=c\,(x_j-x_i)$, with $c=1$ if the target was reached and $c=1-p$ otherwise.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Both seats face the same pool $X$. If $X\ge T$, $u_i-u_j=(e-x_i)-(e-x_j)=x_j-x_i$. If $X<T$, $u_i-u_j=(1-p)\big((e-x_i)-(e-x_j)\big)=(1-p)(x_j-x_i)$.
\end{proof}

The lottery is drawn once per table, so the same ordering holds for realised payoffs: if the catastrophe strikes, both seats get 0, and otherwise the seat that paid less keeps more. The analysis code checks the identity against the game engine's own accounts in all \MixGames{} mixed games, with a largest error below $10^{-12}$.

\subsection{The optimum and the three ways to lose}
\label{sec:loss}

For a game at risk $p$, a model's loss is $\mathrm{opt}(p)-\bar u$, where $\bar u$ is the mean expected payoff of its seats. In self-play all six seats hold the same model, so $\bar u = e-X/6$ if the target is reached and $(1-p)(e-X/6)$ otherwise. The paper splits the loss of each game into three non-negative parts that add up to it:
\begin{align*}
\text{paying past 120} &= \begin{cases}(X-T)/6 & \text{if } X\ge T,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise;}\end{cases}\\
\text{paying below } \popt &= \begin{cases}\mathrm{opt}(p)-(e-T/6) & \text{if } p<\popt \text{ and } X\ge T,\\ \mathrm{opt}(p)-\bar u & \text{if } p<\popt \text{ and } X<T,\\ 0 & \text{if } p\ge\popt;\end{cases}\\
\text{missing the target} &= \begin{cases}\mathrm{opt}(p)-\bar u & \text{if } p\ge\popt \text{ and } X<T,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\end{align*}
Below the pivot, a game that reaches the target loses the difference between keeping everything and paying the fair share, plus any overpayment. At or above the pivot, a game that reaches the target loses only its overpayment, and a game that misses loses everything it could have kept beyond the lottery. The shares reported in the paper are sums of each part over a model's games divided by its total loss.

\subsection{How much risk aversion would explain paying at low risk}
\label{sec:crra}

Take a player with constant relative risk aversion $\gamma$, whose utility of final cash $w$ is $w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ for $\gamma<1$, and compare two plans: pay the fair share and reach the target, which leaves 20 for certain, or pay nothing and miss, which leaves 40 with probability $1-p$ and 0 otherwise. The player prefers the certain 20 if and only if $20^{1-\gamma}\ge(1-p)\,40^{1-\gamma}$, that is
\[
\gamma \;\ge\; 1-\log_2\frac{1}{1-p}.
\]
At $p=0.1$ this needs $\gamma$ of at least \GridCrraLow{}. For $\gamma\ge1$ the utility of 0 is unbounded below, so any positive risk makes the certain 20 preferable; this is why the paper speaks of strong risk aversion. At $p=0$ the two plans leave 20 and 40 for certain, and no increasing utility prefers 20.

\paragraph{When the outcome is settled.} Paying is dominated once the history shows that more payment cannot change the outcome. \subsection{How a round is played}

In each round every seat that holds a model receives its own prompt in a separate call with no memory of earlier calls. The prompt contains the rules, the seat's position, the current round, the seat's remaining money and every seat's contribution in every earlier round, listed by fixed position (Section~\ref{sec:prompts}). The six calls of a round see the same history, so decisions are simultaneous. The running pool is not printed. No seat is given a persona, and all prompts are in English.

Sampling uses temperature 0.7, or 0 in the temperature-zero condition, and an output limit of 3,000 tokens. Each call carries a sampling seed derived from the game, the seat and the round. The service accepts the seed but does not guarantee identical text, so replaying a game does not reproduce its transcripts (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).

\subsection{How replies are read}
\label{sec:parse}

The move is read from the last line of the reply that starts with \texttt{CONTRIBUTION:} and carries one of the allowed amounts. A reply without such a line falls back to the last allowed amount it names. Two situations trigger a new request, with a fresh sampling seed, up to three times: a reply that names no allowed amount at all, and a reply whose length reached the output limit. A reply that reached the limit is asked again with a four times larger limit, up to 8,000 tokens, because asking again with the same limit cuts the reply at the same place; such a reply is never read by the fallback, since any number in it may come from unfinished reasoning. A reply that is still unusable after the retries is counted as unreadable. The data record, for every seat and game, how many replies were unreadable or lacked a \texttt{CONTRIBUTION:} line. Across all \CntDecisions{} model decisions there are \CntParseFail{} such replies, both under the first rewording (Table~\ref{tab:design}), so every other move comes from a \texttt{CONTRIBUTION:} line and the fallback plays no role in the results.

In-game questions are read the same way from a final \texttt{ANSWER:} line (Section~\ref{sec:questions}). An unreadable answer counts as wrong.

\subsection{The lottery and why the paper uses expected payoffs}
\label{sec:lottery}

In the game engine, the catastrophe lottery of a game is one uniform draw that depends only on the repetition index, and a missed game ends in catastrophe if the draw is below $p$. The whole data set therefore uses the same ten draws, in every cell, model and design (Table~\ref{tab:lottery}), and all ten fall below \LotMaxDraw{}. As a result, every missed game at $p\ge0.5$ ended in catastrophe, and missed games at low risk ended in catastrophe more often than the risk implies: in the self-play grid, \SupLotMissed{} games missed the target and \LotRealCat{} of them ended in catastrophe, against \LotExpCat{} expected from their risk levels. Realised payoffs would therefore punish models that miss the target more than chance would. The agents never see the draw, so their play is not affected, and every payoff in the paper is the expected payoff \eqref{eq:ev} given the contributions.

\subsection{Statistical methods}
\label{sec:stats}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unit.} The game. Seats of one game share a history and a lottery, so they are not independent. A model's value in a game is the mean over the seats it holds.
\item \textbf{Intervals.} 95\% percentile bootstrap over games with 5,000 resamples. When a quantity pools several risk levels, games are resampled within each risk level, so each level keeps its weight. For a ratio, such as the share of $\mathrm{opt}(p)$ lost, numerator and denominator are resampled together.
\item \textbf{Tests.} Two-sided permutation tests on the difference in means, with 5,000 permutations, reporting $(h+1)/(n+1)$ for $h$ permuted differences at least as large as the observed one out of $n$. When two conditions are compared over several risk levels, condition labels are permuted within each risk level. The paper marks a prompt change in bold when the shift is at least 2 units and $P<0.01$.
\item \textbf{Exact sign-flip tests.} For Qwen3-235B against its tablemates in each cell of ten games, the one-sided $P$ is the share of all $2^{10}$ sign patterns of the per-game payoff gaps whose mean is at most the observed mean.
\item \textbf{Variance shares.} For the scripted design, the variance of a model's total over the three fixed-amount partner kinds, five risk levels and ten games is split with balanced two-way sums of squares into partner kind, risk, their interaction and a residual.
\item \textbf{Selection.} Uncertainty comes from 1,000 bootstrap replicates that resample the games of every mixed cell and every self-play cell before the whole selection analysis is recomputed (Section~\ref{sec:selection}).
\item \textbf{Seeds.} Every resampling uses a fixed random seed, so rerunning the analysis code reproduces every number in the paper and in this supplement exactly.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

We release the prompts, configurations, seeds and code. The design is reproducible: the lottery draws and the seat layouts depend only on the repetition index and the cell. Model text is not. The model service accepts a sampling seed but does not return identical text for it, and even temperature zero is not deterministic: a ten-game cell at temperature zero contained \RobTempZeroDistinct{} distinct game records on average, against \RobTempZeroBaseDistinct{} at temperature 0.7, where two games count as the same only if all six seats made the same ten moves. Replaying a cell is therefore a new sample of the same design, not a copy of the old one.

\section{Selection among models}
\label{sec:selection}

This section supports Section 7 of the paper and its Figure 7. It gives the method in full, the payoffs that feed it, and every selection setting we computed.

\subsection{Method}

\paragraph{Payoff functions.} For two models $A$ and $B$ and a risk level $p$, $\pi_A(k)$ is the mean over games of the mean expected payoff of the $A$ seats at a table with $k$ seats of $A$ and $6-k$ seats of $B$. For $1\le k\le5$ it comes from the mixed tables (ten games per value). For $k=6$ it is $A$'s self-play payoff at the same risk level (20 games, pooling the base grid and the rerun). Tables~\ref{tab:sel-payoff-01} to~\ref{tab:sel-payoff-09} list every value.

\paragraph{Fitness.} A population holds $N$ agents, $i$ of type $A$ and $N-i$ of type $B$, and tables of six are drawn at random without replacement. An $A$ agent sits with $k-1$ other $A$ agents among its five tablemates with the hypergeometric probability $\mathcal{H}(k-1;N-1,i-1)$, and a $B$ agent sits with $k$ $A$ agents with probability $\mathcal{H}(k;N-1,i)$, where
\[
\mathcal{H}(k;\,M,\,m)=\binom{m}{k}\binom{M-m}{5-k}\Big/\binom{M}{5}.
\]
The fitness of each type is its payoff averaged over these tables, as in Equation (2) of the paper. With $N=6$ the whole population is a single table, so the $A$ agents earn exactly $\pi_A(i)$ and the $B$ agents $\pi_B(6-i)$.

\paragraph{Fixation.} Under the pairwise comparison rule, an agent copies another with probability $1/(1+e^{-\beta\Delta f})$, where $\Delta f$ is the other agent's fitness minus its own. The probability that one $A$ newcomer takes over a population of $B$ is
\[
\rho_{A\to B}=\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\prod_{i=1}^{j}e^{-\beta\,(f_A(i)-f_B(i))}\right)^{-1}.
\]
A takeover is favoured by selection when $\rho_{A\to B}>1/N$, the probability for a neutral newcomer.

\paragraph{$\alpha$-Rank.} In the limit where a new model enters rarely, the population almost always holds a single model. From a population of one model, a newcomer of each of the four other models arrives with equal probability and takes over with the probability above. These transitions form a Markov chain over the five one-model populations, and its stationary distribution is the \emph{stationary mass} of each model. The population's target rate and payoff per seat are the self-play target rates and payoffs weighted by these masses. Figure 7 of the paper is drawn with EGTtools, and the analysis code checks that EGTtools and its own implementation give the same fixation probabilities and masses to within $10^{-7}$. For the two strongest-selection settings at $N=60$ and $N=120$ at $p=0.1$, the eigenvector method sees two unit eigenvalues because some transition probabilities are too small to represent; an exact computation in log space gives the same masses.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.} Each of \SupSelBootReps{} bootstrap replicates resamples the games of every mixed cell and every self-play cell, rebuilds all payoff functions and repeats the whole analysis. We report how often the winner of the point estimate stays on top, and percentile intervals for the population's target rate and payoff.
