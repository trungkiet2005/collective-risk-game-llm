TITLE
Cooperating by Default: Language-Model Agents, Stakes, Partners, and Selection in a Collective-Risk Dilemma

ABSTRACT
Language-model agents increasingly act for people in shared settings, where a group avoids a common loss only if each agent gives up some of its own resources. We ask whether such agents cooperate because of the stakes, because of their partners, or by default. Five low-cost production models play a collective-risk social dilemma, in which six players pay into a shared pool over ten rounds and a catastrophe destroys all savings with a given probability if the pool misses a target. Four models pay about an equal share at every positive risk level, and four of the five also pay at zero risk, where paying cannot help. Stating that only the agent's own cash matters reduces zero-risk payment for most models; removing normative wording does not. Several models correctly say, in separate questions, which choice pays more at each risk level, yet barely change what they pay. Against fixed partners, no model consistently plays the best response, and in mixed tables one late under-contributor can make an otherwise successful group fail. Under rare-mutation imitation with base-prompt payoffs, a population of one table favours the smallest payer and lowers group success, whereas a larger population favours an exact fair-share player at moderate and high risk. Evaluations should state the agent's objective, compare play with a computed optimum, include situations where paying is dominated, and test mixed populations.

KEYWORDS
LLM agents, collective-risk social dilemma, cooperation, best response, empirical game-theoretic analysis, multiagent evaluation
