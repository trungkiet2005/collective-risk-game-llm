TITLE
Paying When It Cannot Help: LLM Agents in a Collective-Risk Dilemma

ABSTRACT
Language-model agents increasingly act on behalf of people in settings where multiple agents share a common outcome. In many such settings, avoiding a collective loss requires each agent to give up some of its own resources. We ask whether such agents adjust their cooperation to the stakes and their partners, or instead follow a largely fixed default behaviour. Five production models play a collective-risk social dilemma in which six players pay into a shared pool over ten rounds; if the pool misses its target, a catastrophe destroys all remaining savings with a given probability. Four models pay about an equal share at every positive risk level, and four of the five also pay at zero risk, where paying cannot help. Explicitly stating that only the agent's own cash matters reduces payment at zero risk for most models; simply making the prompt more neutral does not. Several models correctly identify which choice gives them the higher expected payoff at each risk level, yet barely change what they actually pay. Against fixed partners, no model consistently chooses the payoff-maximising best response, and in groups mixing different models, one agent that underpays in the final round can make an otherwise successful group fail. In payoff-based evolutionary selection, population size changes which model is favoured: with only one six-agent group, selection favours the model that pays least and reduces group success, whereas in larger randomly mixed populations it favours an exact fair-share model at moderate and high risk.

KEYWORDS
LLM agents, collective-risk social dilemma, cooperation, best response, empirical game-theoretic analysis, multiagent evaluation
